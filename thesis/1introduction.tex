For a robot, our world is a messy place, consisting of a multitude of variables, all influencing what a correct behavior would
be at any given moment. Rightfully, newer, larger controller models have equated to better (at least theoretical) performance~\cite{Nauman2024Bigger,Lee2025SimBa,Dehghani2023Scaling}.
However, when transferring them over from training in a digital environment to an actual embodiment, not only do limitations
in memory space and energy usage arise~\cite[p.~7]{Roy2021MachineLearning}, but the real world also behaves slightly differently, which the
controller needs to be robust against~\cite{Tobin2017Domain,Peng2018SimToReal}. So ideally, we should
remove unnecessary parameters, while still maintaining those helping us adapt to variable environments. This leaves us with the question:
\emph{How does enforcing low-dimensional structures in controllers for embodied agents affect their performance and robustness to noise?}

Inspired by nature, it has been shown that more complex behavior can be expressed by simple control architectures if combined with
the right embodiment~\cite{McGeer1990Passive,Mueller2017Morphological}. However, finding this simple architecture remains difficult
as an embodiment might offer a large number of sensors and actuators, which outnumber the dimension governing the underlying dynamics~\cite{Todorov2002Optimal,Lesort2018State}.
Synergizing these, like in nature~\cite{DAvella2003Combinations}, is thus the goal here.
And while low-dimensional controllers for embodied agents have been studied before~\cite{Montufar2015CheapControl,Datar2025Universal},
robustness of such has, to our knowledge, not yet been investigated systematically starting from 
simple linear controllers and transitioning over more complex reinforcement learning based ones
towards perception-based agents acting in simulated environments.
This is where we aim to contribute to.

To be able to quantify performance and what optimal means, we rely on a very well-researched example~\cite{Kalman1960Contributions,Hu2023Toward}: the linear quadratic regulator, which,
together with some related work, we will introduce in \cref{chap:background}.
We use the also well-known double integrator as our environment, and artificially produce higher input and output dimensions via projection for our
controllers to train on, to simulate overparameterization like mentioned above in \cref{chap:lqr}. Then, we will introduce noise to the environment during testing
and compare the robustness between them. The next step consists of moving from these simpler, gain-matrix-based controllers
to reinforcement learning (RL) approaches based on proximal policy optimization~\cite{Schulman2017Proximal} (\cref{chap:inflation}), while still
maintaining our optimal controller from before as the comparison. Robustness evaluations are a part of this again. Finally, we move to an
example closer to reality in \cref{chap:perception}, by introducing a real simulated environment, still based on the double integrator,
which however will be observed by a camera, and influenced by multiple thrusters, which differ from each other. Again introducing noise
in a second step here, this allows us to understand the behavior of low-dimensional controllers better, as we slowly increase the complexity
of the setup throughout. We end this thesis in \cref{chap:conclusion} by discussing our findings, pointing out limitations of our work, before outlining possible
future directions.
