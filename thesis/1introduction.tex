\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chap:introduction}

The human brain contains an estimated 86 billion neurons~\cite{Azevedo2009Equal}, more than five orders of magnitude more
than the roughly 167,000 neurons in the entire central nervous system of a fruit fly~\cite{Berg2026Sexual}.
One might now argue that a human obviously has a much more complex body to govern, and therefore needs this capacity.
Yet a fruit fly achieves stable flight with only a fraction of these neurons, something a human cannot do without help. Size alone,
it seems, does not define a controller's abilities.

In robotics, however, the trend points the other way: newer, larger models have led to better (at least theoretical) performance,
both in vision~\cite{Dehghani2023Scaling} and in continuous control~\cite{Nauman2024Bigger,Lee2025SimBa}.
Yet, when transferring them from training in a digital environment to an actual embodiment, not only do limitations
in memory space and energy usage arise~\cite[p.~7]{Roy2021MachineLearning}, but the real world also behaves slightly differently, which the
controller needs to be robust against~\cite{Tobin2017Domain,Peng2018SimToReal}. So ideally, we should
remove unnecessary parameters, while still maintaining enough to help us adapt to variable environments. This leaves us with the question:
\emph{How does enforcing low-dimensional structures in controllers for embodied agents affect their performance and robustness to noise?}

Inspired by nature, research has shown that complex behavior can be expressed by simple control architectures if combined with
the right embodiment~\cite{McGeer1990Passive,Mueller2017Morphological}. However, finding this simple architecture remains difficult
as an embodiment might offer a large number of sensors and actuators, which outnumber the dimension governing the underlying dynamics~\cite{Todorov2002Optimal,Lesort2018State}.
Synergizing parameters, like muscles do in nature~\cite{DAvella2003Combinations}, is thus the goal for a controller.
And while low-dimensional control of embodied agents has been studied before~\cite{Montufar2015CheapControl,Datar2025Universal},
the robustness of such controllers has, to our knowledge, not yet been investigated systematically, starting from
simple linear controllers, moving on to more complex ones based on reinforcement learning (RL),
and ending with perception-based agents acting in simulated environments.
This is where we aim to contribute the following:

\begin{enumerate}
	\item a systematic comparison of low-dimensional linear quadratic regulator (LQR) solvers with known and estimated models under four noise channels of varying intensities;
	\item an extension of it to RL, evaluating linear, bottleneck and encoder architectures based on proximal policy optimization (PPO)~\cite{Schulman2017Proximal};
	\item a perception-based environment with a camera observing a mass moved by redundant thrusters.
\end{enumerate}

To be able to quantify performance and optimality, we rely on the very well-researched LQR~\cite{Kalman1960Contributions,Hu2023Toward}, which,
together with some related work, we will introduce in \cref{chap:background}. We use the well-known double integrator~\cite{Rao2001Naive} as our environment,
and artificially inflate it to higher input and output dimensions via a lift and a projection for our controllers to train on, to simulate overparameterization.
In \cref{chap:lqr}, we compare LQR-based approaches on this system, followed by introducing noise to the environment during testing
and comparing the robustness between the trained controllers. The next step consists of moving from these simpler, gain-matrix-based controllers
to RL approaches based on PPO~\cite{Schulman2017Proximal} (\cref{chap:inflation}), while still
maintaining our optimal controller from before as the comparison. Robustness evaluations are a part of this again. Finally, in \cref{chap:perception},
we inflate the system even further to dimensions matching our next experiment. Through a simulated environment, still based on the double integrator,
which will be observed by a camera and influenced by multiple differently angled thrusters, we introduce more realism. We again introduce noise
in a second step here, which allows us to better understand the behavior of low-dimensional controllers throughout our experiments.
We conclude in \cref{chap:conclusion} with our findings that low-dimensional structures are difficult to implement
with a performance gain and often lead to worse results without improving robustness to noise, which,
however, could be caused by our controller design. We also discuss the
limitations of our work and propose future experiments which address those.
