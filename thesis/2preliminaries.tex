\chapter{Preliminaries}
\label{chap:preliminaries}
 
Before turning to the experiments, this chapter revisits the concepts that all
later parts of this thesis build on. \Cref{sec:statespace} introduces linear
state-space models and their discretization, \cref{sec:doubleintegrator}

\section{Linear State-Space Models}
\label{sec:statespace}
 
In control theory, the evolution of an agent and together with its environment is
described by a \emph{state-space model}: a rule that assigns to every point of
a state space the instantaneous rate at which the system moves through it.
Throughout this thesis we restrict attention to the class of
\emph{linear time-invariant} (LTI) systems, for which this rule is linear in
both the state and the input and does not itself depend on
time\needcite{ControlSystemsTheoryAndDesign.pdf}.
 
A continuous-time LTI system is given by

\begin{subequations}
\label{eq:ss_cont}
\begin{align}
    \dot{x}(t) &= A_c\, x(t) + B_c\, u(t), \qquad x(0) = x_0, \label{eq:ss_cont_state}\\
    y(t)       &= C\, x(t) + D\, u(t), \label{eq:ss_cont_output}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}

where $x(t) \in \R^{n}$ is the \emph{state vector}, $u(t) \in \R^{m}$ the
\emph{input vector} chosen by the agent, and $y(t) \in \R^{q}$ the
\emph{output vector}, i.e.\ the output observed and thus available to the
feedback controller. Accordingly, $A_c \in \R^{n \times n}$ is called the
\emph{system matrix}, $B_c \in \R^{n \times m}$ the \emph{input matrix},
$C \in \R^{q \times n}$ the \emph{output matrix}, and $D \in \R^{q \times m}$
the \emph{feedthrough matrix}.
 
The two equations in \eqref{eq:ss_cont} play fundamentally different roles, and
it is helpful explaining their distinction.
\Cref{eq:ss_cont_state} is a differential equation: it does not compute the
state, which is assumed to be known at every instant, but prescribes its
\emph{rate of change}. The pair $(A_c, B_c)$ therefore governs how the system
\emph{behaves}, with $A_c$ describing its autonomous dynamics and
$B_c$ describing how the input of the agent influences them. \Cref{eq:ss_cont_output}, in contrast, 
contains no derivative at all. It is an instantaneous readout, and the pair $(C, D)$ therefore
describes how the system is \emph{observed} rather than how it evolves. We set
$D = 0$ throughout, which is the standard assumption for systems in which the
actuator has no direct instantaneous influence on the sensor\needcite{either same notes or textbook}.
 
Given an input signal $u(\cdot)$, the trajectory of \eqref{eq:ss_cont_state} is
obtained by integration and is given in closed form by the variation-of-constants
formula

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:variation_of_constants}
    x(t) = e^{A_c t} x_0 + \int_{0}^{t} e^{A_c (t - s)} B_c\, u(s)\, \mathrm{d}s ,
\end{equation}

where $e^{A_c t}$ denotes the matrix exponential\needcite{same textbook}. This
makes the interpretation of $A_c$ precise: in the scalar case
$\dot{x} = a x$ yields $x(t) = e^{at} x_0$, so $a$ is a growth \emph{rate}
rather than a value. \Cref{eq:variation_of_constants} generalises this, with the
eigenvalues of $A_c$ acting as growth rates along the corresponding
eigendirections.
 
\paragraph{Full state measurement.}
Generally, we assume that the state is fully
measurable and therefore set $C = I_n$, which reduces
\eqref{eq:ss_cont_output} to $y(t) = x(t)$. However, when we introduce disturbances
through noise, this will no longer be the case. More details on this will be
given in \cref{sec:uncertainty}. The distinction between $u = -Kx$ and $u = -Ky$ is
therefore critical, since the former admits an optimal solution while the
latter might not; we return to this point in \cref{sec:lqr}.

\subsection{Discretization}
\label{subsec:discretization}
 
Digital controllers, as in our case, do not act continuously: they sample the output at a fixed
rate and hold the computed controlinput constant until the next sampling point. The
learning algorithms studied in this thesis operate on such sampled trajectories
as well, so our model has to reflect this. The standard approach is the
\emph{zero-order hold} (ZOH)\needcite{find reference}. Fixing a sampling period $h > 0$ and writing
$x_k \coloneqq x(kh)$, the input is assumed to be piecewise constant,

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:zoh_input}
    u(t) = u_k \qquad \text{for } t \in [kh,\, (k+1)h), \quad k \in \mathbb{N}_0 .
\end{equation}

Substituting \eqref{eq:zoh_input} into \eqref{eq:variation_of_constants} over a
single sampling interval and applying the change of variables
$\tau = (k+1)h - s$ yields the discrete-time model

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:ss_disc}
    x_{k+1} = A_d\, x_k + B_d\, u_k ,
\end{equation}

with

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:zoh_matrices}
    A_d = e^{A_c h},
    \qquad
    B_d = \left( \int_{0}^{h} e^{A_c \tau} \, \mathrm{d}\tau \right) B_c .
\end{equation}

Two clarifications need to be made. First, \eqref{eq:ss_disc} is not an approximation: it
reproduces the continuous-time trajectory exactly at the sampling
instants, provided the input is genuinely piecewise constant. This distinguishes
ZOH from other schemes such as the forward Euler discretization
$A_d \approx I + h A_c$, $B_d \approx h B_c$, which is only first-order
accurate.\footnote{For the double integrator of
\cref{sec:doubleintegrator} the two approaches happen to agree on $A_d$ but
\emph{not} on $B_d$, where forward Euler gives $[0,\ h]^{\top}$ instead of the
correct $[h^2/2,\ h]^{\top}$.} Second, the left-hand side of \eqref{eq:ss_disc} is the actual next
state rather than a derivative. The identity
$(x_{k+1} - x_k)/h \approx \dot{x}(kh)$ recovers the continuous picture, and the
identity block contained in $A_d$ but not in $A_c$ is exactly the state carried
over from the previous step.
 
Unless stated otherwise, all experiments in this thesis are carried out in
discrete time with sampling period $h = 0.01$, and
we drop the subscript $d$ in the following, writing simply $A$ and $B$.
 
\section{The Double Integrator}
\label{sec:doubleintegrator}
 
To make the comparisons across all chapters
meaningful, all experiments are built on a single system: the
\emph{double integrator}, a frictionless point mass of mass $m$ moving along a
line under an applied force $u$\needcite{find some reference}. Its dynamics are Newton's second law,

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:newton}
    m \, \ddot{p}(t) = u(t),
\end{equation}

where $p(t)$ denotes the position of the mass. We simplify by using $m = 1$, so that
force and acceleration coincide numerically. Choosing position and velocity as
the state, $x = [\,p,\ v\,]^{\top} \in \R^{2}$, the second-order equation
\eqref{eq:newton} decomposes into the first-order system

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:di_first_order}
    \dot{p}(t) = v(t),
    \qquad
    \dot{v}(t) = u(t),
\end{equation}

in which the first equation is the definition of velocity and the second is
\eqref{eq:newton} itself. In the notation of \eqref{eq:ss_cont} this gives

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:di_cont_matrices}
    A_c = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix},
    \qquad
    B_c = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix},
    \qquad
    C = I_2, \qquad D = 0 .
\end{equation}

The entry $(A_c)_{12} = 1$ should be read as stated in \cref{sec:statespace}:
the rate of change of position is given by the current velocity.
 
Because $A_c^{2} = 0$, the matrix exponential in
\eqref{eq:zoh_matrices} truncates after two terms, $e^{A_c h} = I + A_c h$, and
the discretization is available in closed form:

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:di_disc_matrices}
    A_d = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & h \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix},
    \qquad
    B_d = \begin{bmatrix} h^{2}/2 \\ h \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}

This system is the minimal nontrivial choice for our purposes. It is
second-order, so the state is not directly actuated and the controller must
account for momentum; it is controllable for every $h > 0$; and it is not
open-loop stable, since $A_c$ has a double eigenvalue at the origin and $A_d$
correspondingly a double eigenvalue at $1$. The last property matters for the
learning experiments: an uncontrolled or badly initialised policy diverges, so
the assumption of a known stabilising initial gain (\cref{sec:lqr}) is a real
restriction. These properties are made precise in
\cref{sec:characteristics}.


\section{Characteristics of a system}
\label{sec:characteristics}

Now that we have defined our system, before we can optimize over it, it
is important to clear up different characteristics of the same. The three we
need are properties of the model and not of any particular controller.
Stability is what the controller has to establish in the first place,
controllability is what guarantees that it can, and observability is what
allows a controller that only sees $y$ to act as if it actually recorded $x$.

\subsection{Stability}
\label{subsec:stability}

The first of these is stability, which guarantees that deviations in the system
don't become too drastic, and thus cause some kind of failure. Mathematically,
stability is a statement about a \emph{solution} of the recursion
$x_{k+1} = f(x_k, k)$, and is defined the following
way\needcite{Astrom \& Wittenmark, "Computer-Controlled Systems", p.77}:

\begin{definition}[Stability]
\label{def:stability}
Let $x^{0}(\cdot)$ be a solution of $x_{k+1} = f(x_k, k)$. The solution
$x^{0}(\cdot)$ is called \emph{stable} if for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there
exists a $\delta(\varepsilon, k_0) > 0$ such that every solution $x(\cdot)$ with
$\lVert x(k_0) - x^{0}(k_0) \rVert < \delta$ satisfies
\begin{equation*}
    \lVert x(k) - x^{0}(k) \rVert < \varepsilon
    \qquad \text{for all } k \ge k_0 .
\end{equation*}
It is called \emph{asymptotically stable} if it is stable and in addition
$\lVert x(k) - x^{0}(k) \rVert \to 0$ as $k \to \infty$.
\end{definition}

In the definition, $\varepsilon$ bounds how far a perturbed
trajectory is allowed to deviate, and $\delta$ says how small the initial
distance must be to stay inside that bound. Asymptotic additionally requires 
the deviation to vanish with increasing time.

For linear systems this simplifies considerably. If $x(\cdot)$ and $x^{0}(\cdot)$
both solve $x_{k+1} = A x_k$, then their difference solves the same recursion,
so the behaviour of \emph{one} solution decides the behaviour of all of them.
Stability is thus a property of the system, explicitly the matrix $A$. 
This is stated as a theorem by\needcite{Astrom \& Wittenmark, "Computer-Controlled Systems", p.77}:

\begin{theorem}[Asymptotic stability of discrete-time LTI systems]
\label{thm:asymptotic_stability}
The discrete-time LTI system $x_{k+1} = A x_k$ is asymptotically stable if and
only if all eigenvalues of $A$ lie strictly inside the unit disk, i.e.
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:spectral_radius}
    \spr(A) \coloneqq \max_{i} \lvert \lambda_i(A) \rvert < 1 
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

Our double integrator sits precisely on the boundary. From
\eqref{eq:di_disc_matrices}, $A_d$ has the double eigenvalue $\lambda_{1,2} = 1$,
so $\spr(A_d) = 1$ and \cref{thm:asymptotic_stability} fails. It is not only
non-asymptotically stable but actually unstable. We can see that in the matrix
powers
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:di_powers}
    A_d^{\,k} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & k h \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix},
\end{equation}

which are growing without bound in $k$. This is also logical from a physical
point of view as a frictionless mass with a nonzero initial velocity continues 
its trajectory forever if nothing acts on it. 
Every controller we construct therefore has to \emph{create} stability rather than preserve it.

\subsection{Controllability}
\label{subsec:controllability}

Whether a system is controllable or not indicates if, independently from the
controller, it is possible to reach any state inside its state space in a given
time. A more formal definition is given in
\needcite{ControlSystemsTheoryAndDesign}:

\begin{definition}[Controllability]
\label{def:controllability}
The system is said to be \emph{controllable} if for any initial state
$x_0$ and any final state $x_f$ there exist a horizon $N$ and inputs
$u_0, \dots, u_{N-1}$ such that the solution of \eqref{eq:ss_disc} satisfies
$x_N = x_f$. Otherwise, the system is said to be \emph{uncontrollable}.
\end{definition}

To check whether a given system is actually controllable, we need to compute the
\emph{controllability matrix}\needcite{ControlSystemsTheoryAndDesign}

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:controllability_matrix}
    \mathcal{C}(A, B) =
    \begin{bmatrix} B & AB & A^{2}B & \cdots & A^{n-1}B \end{bmatrix}
    \in \R^{n \times nm},
\end{equation}

and then verify that it has full rank $n$; the system is controllable if and
only if it does\needcite{ControlSystemsTheoryAndDesign}. Note that \eqref{eq:controllability_matrix} is stated for a general
input matrix $B$; for a single input ($m = 1$) it reduces to the square
matrix $[\,b\ \ Ab\ \cdots\ A^{n-1}b\,]$.

Applying this to our double integrator, we obtain

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:di_controllability}
    \mathcal{C}(A_d, B_d) =
    \begin{bmatrix} B_d & A_d B_d \end{bmatrix}
    = \begin{bmatrix} h^{2}/2 & 3h^{2}/2 \\ h & h \end{bmatrix},
    \qquad
    \det \mathcal{C}(A_d, B_d) = -h^{3},
\end{equation}

which is nonzero for every sampling period $h > 0$. The system is therefore
controllable at every sampling rate, as claimed in
\cref{sec:doubleintegrator}.

For \cref{sec:lqr} we will not need the strong \cref{def:controllability}
but can rather suffice with the weaker notion of \emph{stabilizability}
\needcite{ControlSystemsTheoryAndDesign}:

\begin{definition}[Stabilizability]
\label{def:stabilizability}
The pair $(A, B)$ is called \emph{stabilizable} if there exists a gain
$K \in \R^{m \times n}$ such that $\spr(A - BK) < 1$, i.e.\ if the closed loop
obtained from \eqref{eq:ss_disc} with $u_k = -K x_k$ can be made asymptotically
stable. Equivalently, $(A,B)$ is stabilizable if and only if every mode of $A$
that is not controllable is already asymptotically stable.
\end{definition}

However, to decide whether our system is stabilizable,
we do not need to construct this specific $K$. The
Popov-Belevitch-Hautus (PBH) test\needcite{TODO: Find fitting}
allows us to employ it as a rank condition on the relevant eigenvalues:

\begin{theorem}[PBH test for stabilizability]
\label{thm:pbh_stabilizability}
The pair $(A, B)$ is stabilizable if and only if
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:pbh_stabilizability}
    \rank \begin{bmatrix} A - \lambda I_n & B \end{bmatrix} = n
    \qquad \text{for all } \lambda \in \C \text{ with } \lvert \lambda \rvert \ge 1 .
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

Comparing \eqref{eq:pbh_stabilizability} with
\eqref{eq:controllability_matrix} shows that the latter
applies the same rank condition as the former, however 
imposed for \emph{every} $\lambda \in \C$. 
Thus, controllability implies stabilizability but not vice versa. 
The difference contains systems which have uncontrollable
directions decaying on their own, and a controller can hence never
influence.

As our double integrator is already controllable by \eqref{eq:di_controllability},
it is therefore also stabilizable.

\subsection{Observability}
\label{subsec:observability}

Finally, we need to take a look at observability. Above we assumed that we can
actually observe the state of the system we are modelling. However, it is much
more realistic that we only collect a recording of it, for example through
sensors, and need to estimate real state of the
system. The notion of observability describes whether we can determine this
state from our control input $u$ and our recorded response $y$ alone
\needcite{ControlSystemsTheoryAndDesign}:

\begin{definition}[Observability]
\label{def:observability}
The system with state-space model \eqref{eq:ss_disc} and output $y_k = C x_k$
is said to be \emph{observable} if the initial state $x_0$ can be uniquely
determined from the time history of the input $u_k$ and the output $y_k$ over a
finite interval. Otherwise, the system is said to be \emph{unobservable}.
\end{definition}

Dual to the controllability matrix, we can derive the \emph{observability
matrix}

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:observability_matrix}
    \mathcal{O}(C, A) =
    \begin{bmatrix} C \\ CA \\ \vdots \\ CA^{n-1} \end{bmatrix}
    \in \R^{pn \times n},
\end{equation}

and make use of the following theorem\needcite{ControlSystemsTheoryAndDesign}:

\begin{theorem}[Observability rank condition]
\label{thm:observability}
The system is observable if and only if the observability matrix
$\mathcal{O}(C, A)$ has full rank $n$.
\end{theorem}

Using \cref{thm:observability} for our double integrator is actually quite simple:
we set $C = I_2$ in \eqref{eq:di_cont_matrices}, so $\mathcal{O}(C, A_d)$
already contains the identity in its first block rows and has full rank. The
system is thus observable.

If we cannot uniquely determine $x_k$ from our observation, we work with an
estimate $\hat{x}_k$ instead. Running a copy of the model,
$\hat{x}_{k+1} = A \hat{x}_k + B u_k$, the estimation error
$\tilde{x}_k \coloneqq x_k - \hat{x}_k$ follows the dynamics

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:estimation_error}
    \tilde{x}_{k+1} = A\, \tilde{x}_k,
    \qquad \tilde{x}_0 = x_0 - \hat{x}_0 ,
\end{equation}

which is again just the system without control input. The decay of the error
therefore depends only on the eigenvalues of $A$.
If the system is unstable, the error grows infinitely\needcite{ControlSystemsTheoryAndDesign} 
and for our example it does so
linearly, by \eqref{eq:di_powers}. Observability fixes this by correcting
the model with the measurement,
$\hat{x}_{k+1} = A \hat{x}_k + B u_k + L (y_k - C \hat{x}_k)$, which turns
\eqref{eq:estimation_error} into $\tilde{x}_{k+1} = (A - LC) \tilde{x}_k$. If
the system is observable, we can choose a $L$ such that the eigenvalues
of $A - LC$ lie inside the unit disk, making it stable.

As with stabilizability, the weaker requirement is called \emph{detectability}, 
and it is the condition we will rely on in \cref{sec:lqr}. 

\begin{definition}[Detectability]
\label{def:detectability}
The pair $(A, C)$ is called \emph{detectable} if there exists an observer gain
$L \in \R^{n \times p}$ such that $\spr(A - LC) < 1$. Equivalently, $(A,C)$ is
detectable if and only if every unobservable mode of $A$ is already
asymptotically stable.
\end{definition}

Detectability is the dual of \cref{def:stabilizability} which gives us
that $(A, C)$ is detectable if and only if $(A\T, C\T)$ is
stabilizable\needcite{ControlSystemsTheoryAndDesign}. This follows as
$\spr(A - LC) = \spr\!\left((A\T - C\T L\T)\right)$. Transposing the test
for stabilizability \eqref{eq:pbh_stabilizability} leaves us with the dual rank test:

\begin{theorem}[PBH test for detectability]
\label{thm:pbh_detectability}
The pair $(A, C)$ is detectable if and only if
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:pbh_detectability}
    \rank \begin{bmatrix} A - \lambda I_n \\ C \end{bmatrix} = n
    \qquad \text{for all } \lambda \in \C \text{ with } \lvert \lambda \rvert \ge 1 .
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

With $C = I_2$ the matrix in \eqref{eq:pbh_detectability} contains an identity
block and has full rank for every $\lambda$, so our system is observable and
therefore detectable. The reason we introduce the weaker notion here is
\cref{thm:dare_existence}, whose hypothesis is the detectability of
$(A, Q^{1/2})$ rather than $(A, C)$. This shifts the focus from what
the sensor sees, to which directions of the state space are penalised by the
cost.

The topic of observability will become important again in
\cref{sec:uncertainty}, where we deliberately introduce noise so that
$y_k \neq x_k$. This doesn't change our matrix $C = I_2$, 
so the rank condition of \cref{thm:observability} still
holds and the system remains observable by definition. However,
$y_k$ is no longer a deterministic function of $x_k$, so we can
never estimate the state exactly. We revisit this and its
implications there.
 
\section{The Linear Quadratic Regulator}
\label{sec:lqr}

Now that we have defined our state-space model and its important
characteristics, it is time to introduce our controller, which is based on the
\emph{linear quadratic regulator} (LQR) problem.

Here, the setting is that our system starts at an arbitrary initial state in its
state space, and the goal of our controller is to return to the zero state as
fast as possible. At the same time, the control input should be limited to a realistic
quantity, such that it could actually be produced by an actuator.
Both requirements can be combined into a single cost function, where $Q$
describes the former, while $R$
associates costs to control input. Over a finite horizon $T$, this gives the
cost\needcite{OptimalAndRobustControl.pdf}

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:lqr_cost_finite}
    V_T(x_0, u) = \int_{0}^{T} \left( x(t)\T Q\, x(t) + u(t)\T R\, u(t) \right) \mathrm{d}t ,
    \qquad Q = Q\T \succeq 0, \quad R = R\T \succ 0 .
\end{equation}

We notice to further restrictions here: $Q$ is required to be positive
semi-definite, meaning deviating from the origin isn't reducing costs,
and $R$ must be positive definite, such that every control input results in a cost.

As the original formula relates to a continuous problem with a fixed endtime $T$,
it doesn't fit well to our goal of evaluating over an infinite discrete horizon.
The following formula offers exactly that:

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:lqr_cost_infinite}
    J(x_0, u) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \left( x_k\T Q\, x_k + u_k\T R\, u_k \right) .
\end{equation}

This sum does not necessarily converge. If the closed loop
does not decay, the costs do not vanish and $J$ tends to $+\infty$. 
Using this as our definition, $J$ becomes well defined for every gain.

\subsection{The optimal gain}
\label{subsec:lqr_optimal}

What makes the LQR problem special is that its solution is a static linear feedback
law: the optimal input at time $k$ depends only the current state of the model.
Introducing a gain matrix $K \in \R^{m \times n}$, we write it as
\footnote{Note that the minus in front of the K has been chosen as the convention for this paper.
This turns our closed loop into the format $A - BK$, while some referenced literature
uses $A + BK$. Formulas cited have been adapted to our convention by replacing $K$ with $-K$.
}

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:feedback_law}
    u_k = -K x_k .
\end{equation}

Our controller is thus reduced to choosing a matrix, and the
optimal controller is given by the optimal gain matrix $K^{*}$.

As we are dealing with a discrete-time system, that gain is obtained from the
\emph{discrete algebraic Riccati equation} (DARE)

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:dare}
    P = A\T P A - A\T P B \left( R + B\T P B \right)^{-1} B\T P A + Q ,
\end{equation}

whose stabilizing solution $P = P\T \succeq 0$ yields

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:optimal_gain}
    K^{*} = \left( R + B\T P B \right)^{-1} B\T P A ,
    \qquad
    u_k^{*} = -K^{*} x_k .
\end{equation}

The matrix $P$ is additioanlly our
\emph{cost-to-go} matrix, meaning that we can use it to calculate the optimal cost 
incurred from any state $x$ onwards with

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:cost_to_go}
    J^{*}(x) = x\T P x .
\end{equation}

Whether \eqref{eq:dare} has such a solution at all is answered by the structural
properties of \cref{sec:characteristics}\needcite{OptimalAndRobustControl.pdf}:

\begin{theorem}[Existence and uniqueness]
\label{thm:dare_existence}
Let $(A, B)$ be stabilizable and let $(A, Q^{1/2})$ be detectable. Then the DARE
\eqref{eq:dare} has a unique symmetric positive semi-definite solution $P$, which
is the only stabilizing one, and the resulting closed loop satisfies
$\spr(A - B K^{*}) < 1$. If $(A, Q^{1/2})$ is observable, $P$ is positive
definite.
\end{theorem}

\subsection{The cost as a function of the gain}
\label{subsec:lqr_cost_of_gain}

\Cref{eq:optimal_gain} solves the problem, but it solves it directly and
tells us nothing about the quality of non-optimal gains. The learning algorithms of
the following chapters never see the model itself; they search over gains
directly, and for that we need the cost as a function of $K$ rather than of an
input sequence.

Fix a gain $K$ and write the resulting closed loop as
$x_{k+1} = A_K x_k$ with $A_K \coloneqq A - BK$. Substituting
\eqref{eq:feedback_law} into \eqref{eq:lqr_cost_infinite} shows that the cost is
again a quadratic form, $J(x_0, K) = x_0\T P_K x_0$, where $P_K$ now solves
the far simpler discrete \emph{Lyapunov equation}

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:lyapunov}
    P_K = A_K\T P_K A_K + Q + K\T R K ,
\end{equation}

which is linear in the unknown $P_K$ and has a unique solution whenever
$\spr(A_K) < 1$. This restricts us to the set of stabilizing gains

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:stabilizing_set}
    \mathcal{K} \coloneqq \left\{ K \in \R^{m \times n} : \spr(A - BK) < 1 \right\} ,
\end{equation}

outside of which we set the cost to $+\infty$, consistent with the remark after
\eqref{eq:lqr_cost_infinite}.

One dependency remains, namely on the initial state. Evaluating the gain
based on a single $x_0$ would reward gains that happen to fit that one
point, so we instead draw the initial state from a distribution,
$x_0 \sim \mathcal{D}$, and average. Writing
$\Sigma \coloneqq \E_{x_0 \sim \mathcal{D}}[x_0 x_0\T]$ for its second moment,
the objective becomes

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:cost_of_gain}
    f(K) \coloneqq \E_{x_0 \sim \mathcal{D}} \left[ x_0\T P_K x_0 \right]
    = \tr \left( P_K \Sigma \right) ,
\end{equation}

using the cyclic property of the trace. \Cref{eq:cost_of_gain} is the object
that every remaining chapter of this thesis minimizes, and the algorithms differ
only in how much of the model is known to them.

Throughout this thesis we set $Q = I_2$ and $R = 1$, which weighs position,
velocity and control effort equally. Initial states are drawn uniformly from the
box $[-10, 10] \times [-1, 1]$.

\section{Uncertainty and Disturbance Models}
\label{sec:uncertainty}

Until now, we assumed that we can fully observe the system, and that its
behaviour is governed by exactly the dynamics we know. However, in reality there
are influences on such systems which we don't clearly know of. Especially for
embodied agents this is important, as the world is a messy place.

We will thus introduce four different disturbances to be included in the model,
commonly known as noise. They are distinguished by the point at which they
affect the loop: what the controller \emph{observes} (measurement noise),
what its output actually \emph{does} (control noise), what the plant
\emph{is} (parametric uncertainty), and what else \emph{acts} on it
(external disturbance). Each of them can be activated separately, so that we
can clearly isolate the cause of an effect. Throughout this section
$k$ indexes the sampling instants, and $\sigma$ denotes the standard deviation
of the respective noise.

\subsection{Measurement Noise}
\label{subsec:measurementnoise}

This one mainly relates to observability: we influence the sensors used to measured
the model state, such that capturing the same state twice would
not lead to the same observation $y_k$. 
Let $n^{\mathrm{m}}_k$ denote the measurement noise at step $k$. Then

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:measurement_noise}
    n^{\mathrm{m}}_k \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(0, \sigma_{\mathrm{m}}^{2} I_n\right),
    \qquad
    y_k = x_k + n^{\mathrm{m}}_k ,
    \qquad \sigma_{\mathrm{m}} \in \R_{+} ,
\end{equation}

drawn independently at every step.

While one might think that this noise removes observability for us, it doesn't.
The output matrix is still $C = I_2$, 
so the rank condition of \cref{thm:observability} holds
unchanged and observability is given. 

However, it does remove the deterministic link between state and
output, so we can't identify $x_k$ exactly with any amount of measurements. Using
our noisy observation in the feedback law \eqref{eq:feedback_law} and $u_k = -K y_k$, 
the closed loop becomes

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:measurement_closed_loop}
    x_{k+1} = (A - BK)\, x_k - BK\, n^{\mathrm{m}}_k .
\end{equation}

So even a perfectly stabilizing $K$ can no longer bring the state exactly to the origin, 
but rather to a distribution around it.

\subsection{Control Noise}
\label{subsec:controlnoise}

Control noise, as the name says, acts on the applied control input. With it,
we try to model thedifference between the intended movement of an actuator,
and its actual action due to different factors. Here we deal with
\emph{multiplicative} noise, which scales with the input. In
detail this means that the applied control input $\tilde{u}_k$ is obtained as

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:control_noise}
    n^{\mathrm{c}}_k \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(0, \sigma_{\mathrm{c}}^{2} I_m\right),
    \qquad
    \tilde{u}_k = \left( 1 + n^{\mathrm{c}}_k \right) \odot u_k ,
\end{equation}

where $\odot$ denotes the elementwise product, so that $\sigma_{\mathrm{c}}$ is
the \emph{relative} error. Applying this to the input, the next step becomes

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:control_noise_step}
    x_{k+1} = A\, x_k + B\, \tilde{u}_k .
\end{equation}

\subsection{Parametric Uncertainty}
\label{subsec:paremetricuncertainty}

This noise acts directly on the matrices of the model. Here, we either decide to keep
it time invariant, meaning $\Delta_k = \Delta_0$ for all $k$, or to resample it
at every step. The former models a wrongly identified system, while the latter
simulates one that continuously changes. As with measurement noise, it is additive. 
Applied, it acts on the system the
following way:

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:parametric_noise}
    \left( \Delta_k^{A} \right)_{ij},\ \left( \Delta_k^{B} \right)_{ij}
    \overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}\!\left(0, \sigma_{\mathrm{p}}^{2}\right),
    \qquad
    x_{k+1} = \left( A + \Delta_k^{A} \right) x_k + \left( B + \Delta_k^{B} \right) \tilde{u}_k .
\end{equation}

Worth noting is that resampling every
step leaves the model correct \emph{on average}, since
$\E[\Delta_k] = 0$, and mainly adds variance to the trajectory. This stands
in contrast to the time invariant version, where we have an discrepancy between
what we compute actions on, and what actually happens.

\subsection{External Disturbance}
\label{subsec:externaldisturbance}

This noise models external influences, like a constant wind pushing on our
agent, or periodic disturbances, for example rain
hitting it with some frequency. Depending on the type, the disturbance
$n^{\mathrm{d}}_k \in \R^{n}$ is drawn as

\begin{subequations}
\label{eq:disturbance_noise}
\begin{align}
    \text{Gaussian:} \quad
        & n^{\mathrm{d}}_k \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(0, \sigma_{\mathrm{d}}^{2} I_n\right)
          \quad \text{independently at every step}, \label{eq:dist_gauss}\\
    \text{Sinusoidal:} \quad
        & n^{\mathrm{d}}_k = \sigma_{\mathrm{d}} \sin(0.01\,k)\, \mathbf{1}_n , \label{eq:dist_sin}\\
    \text{Constant:} \quad
        & n^{\mathrm{d}}_k = n^{\mathrm{d}}_0 \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(0, \sigma_{\mathrm{d}}^{2} I_n\right)
          \quad \text{for all } k . \label{eq:dist_const}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}

Only the first one can really be considered as noise; the other are more
deterministic values, where $\sigma_{\mathrm{d}}$ acts as the amplitude of it. 
The sinusoidal variant completes one period every
$2\pi / 0.1 \approx 63$ steps, which at a sampling intervall of $h = 0.01$ 
is relatively a rather slow drift than a rapid vibration. Including the
noise in our loop, the state transition becomes

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:disturbance_step}
    x_{k+1} = A\, x_k + B\, \tilde{u}_k + n^{\mathrm{d}}_k .
\end{equation}

\subsection{The full loop}
\label{subsec:full_noise}
While we, until now, have only regarded each noise type on their own,
there is also the possibility to include them all at once. Doing so,
a singular step becomes:

\begin{subequations}
\label{eq:noisy_loop}
\begin{align}
    y_k     &= x_k + n^{\mathrm{m}}_k , \label{eq:noisy_obs}\\
    u_k     &= -K y_k , \label{eq:noisy_ctrl}\\
    \tilde{u}_k     &= \left( 1 + n^{\mathrm{c}}_k \right) \odot u_k , \label{eq:noisy_act}\\
    x_{k+1} &= \left( A + \Delta_k^{A} \right) x_k + \left( B + \Delta_k^{B} \right) \tilde{u}_k + n^{\mathrm{d}}_k . \label{eq:noisy_dyn}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}

Relevant to note about \eqref{eq:noisy_loop} is that the controller only 
interacts with $y_k$ and $u_k$, whereas the dynamics
and the stage cost of \eqref{eq:lqr_cost_infinite} are evaluated on the true
state $x_k$. Thus our controller can never actually observe the true state it is
evaluated on. For our baseline LQR solution of \cref{sec:lqr}, which we will use to compare,
we compute the gain on a pure system, and only afterwards apply it to an
\emph{exposed} system of the style \eqref{eq:noisy_loop} afterwards. Measuring how much
performance this difference makes is the subject of the next section.
 
\section{Metrics and Robustness}
\label{sec:robustness}

In this thesis we try to answer two main questions. First, how well a 
trained controller performs generally, as well as under influences
unknown to him during training. The second relates more to the size
of the controller, and a possible tradeoff between performance and
the former.

\subsection{Cost and suboptimality}
\label{subsec:suboptimality}

By \cref{thm:dare_existence} no gain achieves a
lower cost \eqref{eq:cost_of_gain} than $K^{*}$, and every controller we
train is therefore in the best case only as good as it. This serves as our
baseline, and we evaluate the cost produces by our controllers in relation to
it.

While can easily calculate the infinite-horizon cost of a gain
via the Lyapunov equation \eqref{eq:lyapunov}, we can't effectively
use it. The reason for that is that \eqref{eq:lyapunov} can only
be applied to the closed loop, however when adding noise to our
evaluation, this is not the case anymore. Additionally, the neural
networks we will train later on, don't admit a gain matrix based
policy $P_K$, which would need a different approach anyway.

So, we instead evaluate them over a fixed horizon via rollout. Hence for
a given controller, a noise condition $c$, which is a subset of channels and an
intensity \cref{subsec:noise_strength}, and a horizon $T$, we
simulate starting from a randomly drawn $x_0 \sim \mathcal{D}$.
The resulting loop \eqref{eq:noisy_loop} under $c$ thus accumulates

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:empirical_return}
    J_T^{(i,s)}(K; c)
    = \sum_{k=0}^{T-1}
      \left( x_k\T Q\, x_k + \tilde{u}_k\T R\, \tilde{u}_k \right) ,
\end{equation}

where $s$ indexes the seed the run was trained under and $i$ the episode
within it.

Writing $\mathcal{F}$ for the pairs $(s,i)$ whose episode stayed finite,
the cost estimate becomes

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:empirical_cost}
    \hat{J}_T(K; c)
    = \frac{1}{\lvert \mathcal{F} \rvert}
      \sum_{(s,i) \in \mathcal{F}} J^{(i,s)}_T(K; c) ,
    \qquad
    \mathcal{F} = \left\{ (s,i) \;:\; J^{(i,s)}_T(K; c) < \infty \right\} .
\end{equation}

Our final metric is the relative excess of the cost produced by a
trained controller over the cost of the optimal controller $K^{*}$
under the same condition. It is taken per \emph{per episode}, meaning
an episode $(s,i)$ of a trained controller and episode $(s,i)$ of the baseline
are compared here. Both start from the same $x_0$ and are affected by 
the same noise. Overall this yields:

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:suboptimality}
    S^{(i,s)}(K; c)
        = \frac{J^{(i,s)}_T(K; c) - J^{(i,s)}_T(K^{*}; c)}
                {J^{(i,s)}_T(K^{*}; c)} ,
    \qquad
    \hat{S}(K; c)
        = \frac{1}{\lvert \mathcal{F} \rvert}
          \sum_{(s,i) \in \mathcal{F}} S^{(i,s)}(K; c) ,
\end{equation}

where $S = 0$ means that the trained controller performed as well
as our baseline and $S = 0.1$ that it did ten percent worse.

Note that this metric can indeed be negative. $K^{*}$ is only optimal for
the nominal problem \cref{sec:lqr}, which has exact state feedback on
known matrices and no external disturbance. These conditions do not
hold, unless no noise is applied, which is only true if $c=\varnothing$.

\subsection{Test protocol}
\label{subsec:protocol}

For all evaluation throughout this thesis, we will use a standardized
approach, so that only the trained controller varies between different runs.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Seeds.} $M = 10$ fixed seeds, drawn once randomly.
    \item \textbf{Episodes.} $N = 100$ rollouts per seed, each from an
          independently sampled $x_0 \sim \mathcal{D}$.
    \item \textbf{Horizon.} $T = 2000$ steps at $h = 0.01$, so a total
            of $20\,$seconds.
    \item \textbf{Initial states.} Drawn from the box
          $[-10, 10] \times [-1, 1]$ of \cref{sec:lqr}.
    \item \textbf{Initial gain.} For iterative solvers requiring a
            stable initial gain, we use $K_0 = [\,0.1,\ 0.5\,]$,
            whose closed loop has $\spr(A - B K_0) \approx 0.9975$.
\end{itemize}

Under the stronger conditions individual rollouts might diverge. 
To keep our mean finite, we remove them from the mean calculation,
and report them separately as the divergence rate:

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:divergence_rate}
    D(K; c) \coloneqq \frac{1}{M N}
    \left| \left\{ (s, i) \;:\; J^{(i,s)}_T(K; c) = \infty \ \right\} \right| .
\end{equation}

An individual episode can be missing from the result due to another reason. 
Where the \emph{baseline} evaluation of episode $(s,i)$ diverged,
$S^{(i,s)}$ has no value at all, since \eqref{eq:suboptimality} would divide 
by an infinity. Those episodes are excluded from $\hat{S}$ and named seperately.

\subsection{Noise Intensity}
\label{subsec:noise_strength}

\Cref{sec:uncertainty} introduced four channels which change the system
compared to the base model. Each noise channel is seperated further
into five intensities,

\begin{center}
    \emph{none} $\prec$ \emph{weak} $\prec$ \emph{standard} $\prec$
    \emph{strong} $\prec$ \emph{divergent},
\end{center}

where \emph{none} leaves the system as it is and thus serves as our
control condition. All the other intensities were chosen by applying
each channel to the optimal gain $K^{*}$ and observing its deviation
from the optimal path. \emph{divergent}, contrary to the name,
doesn't always cause divergence, but makes the controller unusable. 
The concrete standard deviations per channel are listed in 
\cref{tab:noise_levels}.

After an initial analysis of the influence of the different channels
separately and combined at their different intensities, we will decide on
one of them for the remaining chapters.

\subsection{The size of a policy}
\label{subsec:policy_size}

The second question is one of size. For a neural network the known way 
is simply the number of weights and biases of the actor \needcite{TODO}.
For a gain matrix however, we need to find a definition that is comparable to it.

The simply approach for a $K \in \R^{m \times n}$ is the number of its entries,
$m n$. In \Cref{chap:inflation} however, we will reduce the rank of $K$.
Optimizing the storage size for $K$ of rank $r$, the singular value decomposition
\needcite{SVD\_Notes.pdf} seperates it into

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:svd}
    K = U \Sigma V\T ,
    \qquad
    U \in \R^{m \times r}, \quad
    \Sigma = \operatorname{diag}(\sigma_1, \dots, \sigma_r), \quad
    V \in \R^{n \times r} ,
\end{equation}

with $U$ and $V$ having orthonormal columns and $\sigma_1 \geq \dots \geq
\sigma_r > 0$ being the non-zero singular values. Hence, we only need to
store $mr$ entries for $U$, $r$ for the diagonal of $\Sigma$ and
$rn$ for $V$, which in total yields $r(m + n + 1)$. 
For a small enough $r$ this is less, so we take the minimum of both:

\begin{definition}[Policy size]
\label{def:policy_size}
The size of a linear policy $K \in \R^{m \times n}$ of rank
$r = \rank(K)$ is
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:policy_size}
    \lvert K \rvert \coloneqq \min \left\{ m n, \; r \, (m + n + 1) \right\} ,
\end{equation}
the number of real numbers required to store it.
The size of a neural policy is its total number of trainable parameters in the
actor.
\end{definition}
