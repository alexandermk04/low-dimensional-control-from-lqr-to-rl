\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:motivation}

An agent acting in its environment receives observational input, and responds to it with a commanded
control input.
Differently said, the agent is a function in relation to its environment, and to find an optimal
behavior, we need to approximate this optimal function as closely as possible. While Neural Networks
are known to be universal approximators~\cite{Cybenko1989Approximation,Hornik1989Multilayer}, and
hence able to approximate any function, in the case of an embodied agent, different constraints need to
be taken into account. Ay~\cite{Ay2015Geometric} names additionally to external embodiment constraints
possible intrinsic constraints, and argues that such can even be ``beneficial'' for the control
structure. Additionally, less parameters for deployment are helpful due to energy usage and storage
capacity, the core of the notion of cheap design\needcite{TODO}.
So, for such embodied agents, the goal is to find an ``embodied universal
approximator''~\cite[p.~3]{Montufar2015CheapControl}, which restricts the neural network
architecture to an intrinsic, unknown dimension, encoding physical properties belonging to the agent
and the environment, while disallowing impossible movements~\cite[Sec.~3]{Montufar2015CheapControl}.

Biology motivates this approach even further: Here it is clear to see, that by commanding a body,
some features of the control architecture can be simplified and delegated to the
embodiment\needcite{TODO}. For example, McGeer~\cite{McGeer1990Passive}'s passive walker clearly
shows, that some movements can be produced by the body without any control input, just by choosing
the right embodiment. While obviously here being simplified to not include any control at all, it
can quite simply be extended with such \needcite{\#9, McGeer reference to his previous paper}.
M{\"u}ller and Hoffmann~\cite[p.~4]{Mueller2017Morphological} mention such combination, as a robot
climbing up walls, whose design was inspired by geckos, indeed using energy for control, however
very little compared to an approach not exploiting these specific embodiment design. Further, it can
be seen that the complex
behaviors are not only less energy intensive to produce, but also utilize muscle
synergies~\cite{DAvella2003Combinations} and thus fewer control parameters. And while it is
difficult to clearly attribute the effect of the embodiment~\cite{Zahedi2013Quantifying}, for us,
this gives us the reason to believe that the simplicity, and thus efficiency, comes from a
low-dimensionality of the controller~\cite{Montufar2015CheapControl}.

That this is indeed true for certain examples, has been shown both in theory and practice
before~\cite{Datar2025Universal,Montufar2015Geometry,Li2018Measuring}. Li et
al.~\cite{Li2018Measuring} call this lower-dimension ``intrinsic dimension'', which is the
dimensionality required for the model to be able to find policies. They show for example that 
for achieving 90 percent of the performance of a fully connected network on MNIST,
the dimensionality can be reduced by 99.6 percent. For a convolutional neural
network, it is still a 99.3 percent reduction.
Others show that the ``least complex model which is able to learn
an optimal policy does learn faster''~\cite{Montufar2015Geometry}. For a neural network reducing
parameters can be done in different ways\needcite{TODO}, some of which we will take a look at in
\cref{chap:inflation}. In comparison, for LQR, where the controller is a gain matrix, the
corresponding approach is reducing its rank~\cite{Recht2010Guaranteed}. However, while these
approaches are more parameter efficient while achieving similar, or slightly worse performance, it
was also observed that they tend to perform even worse when encountering
noise~\cite{Datar2025Universal}.

Robustness to noise however, is one of the important characteristics of controllers acting under
real-world conditions~\cite{Rao2001Naive,Roy2021MachineLearning}. One approach of achieving this
in nature is the principle of ``redundancy and diversity''~\cite{Kitano2004Biological}, which stands
in direct contrast to our goal of reducing redundant parameterizations. Another reason for producing
robust controllers is the difficult transfer from digital training environment to real-world
embodiments, commonly referred to as the ``reality gap''~\cite{Tobin2017Domain,Peng2018SimToReal}.
Approaches of training robust controllers typically introduce noise already during training, be it
either by using a LQG or including variations to the dynamics~\cite{Rao2001Naive,Peng2018SimToReal}.
These shown approaches however, don't look at low-dimensionality, which makes sense as
Kitano~\cite[p.~833]{Kitano2004Biological} poses that robustness cannot be increased
without sacrificing performance or accepting ``increased resource
demands''.

Before taking a look at the more complex case of reinforcement learning, we start simpler by
using the simpler, and more well understood Linear Quadratic Regulator. This approach has been taken
before~\cite{Recht2019Tour} and does make sense given the guarantees regarding convergence and
effectiveness of Policy Optimization for it~\cite{Fazel2018Global,Takakura2023Structured}, as well
as the understanding of its geometry~\cite{Talebi2024Policy}.
It also offers methods which produce certain structures in its controller, like sparsity or our desired
low-dimensionality, as given by Structured Policy
Iteration~\cite{Park2020Structured}. Approaches can generally be categorized into two groups if the underlying dynamics
are unknown and only data is available: Estimating the model, and then applying a model-based solver (indirect) or using the data
to estimate the gain matrix (direct)~\cite{Zhao2024DataEnabled}. Both the indirect, as in system identification
with uncertainty bounds~\cite{Dean2020Sample}, and the direct setting, with an $\Hinf$ constraint
that bounds the closed-loop gain from disturbance to output~\cite{Zhang2021Policy},
have been studied in regards to robustness, however to our knowledge not in combination with low-rank policies.

Research evaluating RL on control tasks have also shown that there is room for simplification, for
example using linear policies instead of complex, fully connected neural
networks~\cite{Mania2018Simple,Rajeswaran2017Towards}. This, together with the
fact that parameters in neural networks are highly dependent, and thus redundant~\cite{Denil2013Predicting}, allows us to assume that
low-dimensionality can improve efficiency here. Examples for this exist, for example by factorizing a discretized low-rank
policy into a thin and wide matrix~\cite{Rozada2024Matrix} or by introducing an orthogonal projection inside the layer
structure~\cite{Todorov2026Orthogonal}. While they produce lower dimensionality, a different approach uses ``information bottlenecks'',
which exhibit better generalization behavior in unseen environments~\cite[Sec.~4.2]{Igl2019Generalization},
which again could help us with robustness.

A clear case where low-dimensional dynamics underly a high-dimensional observational space is in perception-based tasks.
Agents observing only images instead of the states of simple control tasks are posed with the challenge of extracting
these states from a high-dimensional, often non-linear encoding~\cite[Sec.~1]{Watter2015Embed}. Many approaches, based on
state representation learning (SRL), exist, which use different variants to create a latent embedding of the image,
used as a state~\cite{Lesort2018State}. The most interesting approach, especially for us looking at embodied agents, is
the usage of so-called ``priors'', which restrict the latent space by using knowledge about the
environment~\cite{Lesort2018State}.
This brings us back to Ay's argument, that the embodiment actively defines the action space~\cite{Ay2015Geometric}.
Robotic priors have been introduced by Jonschkowski and Brock in 2015~\cite{Jonschkowski2015Learning}, and then further
refined by Botteghi et al. for continuous action spaces~\cite{Botteghi2021LowDimensional}. A more specific version
restricted to position and velocity also exists here, training a model to encode an image into a low-dimensional
position state, from which the velocity is calculated by finite differences~\cite{Jonschkowski2017PVE}.
While all that sounds promising, in contrast stand findings hinting that the problems of robust control
also apply to perception-based control, as noise induced through perception directly affects
the performance~\cite{Xu2021Perception} and thus needs approaches for improving
it~\cite{Dean2020Robust}.

Our goal for this thesis is therefore to look a bit deeper at the combination of low-dimensionality
and robustness in an empirical way, motivated by the findings of Datar and Ay~\cite{Datar2025Universal}. LQR
serves as the perfect, well-investigated basis~\cite{Recht2019Tour}, offering low-rank approaches that have been proposed
and analysed theoretically, however not yet been applied in practice and under noise~\cite{Park2020Structured}. Previously
investigated policies for LQR under noise in comparison didn't look at low-dimensionality
specifically~\cite{Zhang2021Policy}.
For reinforcement learning, low-dimensionality has been explored
previously~\cite{Agarwal2020FLAMBE,Stojanovic2024ModelFree}, and also
in comparison with robustness~\cite{Li2025AdaRL}, but doesn't take into account the embodiment we are interested in.
Closing this gap, our main interest is to compare robustness properties for low-dimensional policies for embodied
agents under increasingly more complex, and noisier environments.

which is exactly why we start our investigation here. For our investigations, we will
use the double integrator, as mentioned in \needcite{chap 2}.

Our first approach for chapter 4 is to compare our selected methods across these inflation sizes, and see how increasing
the dimension affects the performance of each. As the next step, we will introduce the different noise channels
named in \needcite{chap 4} first to the base double integrator, where no overparameterization exists. The results
we observe here, will then be compared with evaluations of the controllers for a \emph{largely} inflated system
under different noise channels and strengths.

In \needcite{chap 5} we will then first introduce RL based approaches. With PPO \needcite{TODO} being one of
the most used variants in robotics \needcite{TODO}, we choose this as our main architecture, and apply different
variations of it. First, by doing xyz \needcite{TODO, missing text} we enforce the trained controller to be linear, which allows us to extract 
it as a gain matrix, that we can closely compare with our LQR approaches (ppo_linear). The fully connected, standard version is named
\emph{ppo_mlp}, and will be also evaluated. Additionally, we will introduce structures, that force the observations through a low-dimensional latent
space. Here, we will reuse the N4SID system identifier from before, by creating an encoding matrix from the singular value elimination, that projects
the high-dimensional observation first into the low-dimensionality, and then trains PPO with this as the input. Therefore, the name for it is \emph{ppo_encoder}.
A similar approach is used for \emph{ppo_encoder_scalable}, which is based on \needcite{Jonschkowski2015Learning,Botteghi2021LowDimensional} and uses
robotic priors do derive a low-dimensional latent state, via a trainable encoder. Again, the construction following this encoder is PPO. Lastly, we
implement the proposed orthonormal bottlenecks introduced by Todorov et. al \needcite{Todorov2026Orthogonal}. It consists of sampling a fixed orthonormal matrix
B of rank k, which is added at the end of a trainable feature extractor, and used to project the output into a k-dimensional space. Like with the other
encoders, we follow by our PPO architecture. 

All these low-dimensional approaches, if visualized, exhibit a "bow-tie" shape, which in nature can actually
improve robustness \needcite{Kitano2004Biological}. So this indeed interesting to investigate. However,
first, like in \needcite{chap 4}, we start by comparing these different approaches across the different inflation
sizes, without any noise applied, to see how they generally behave. Then, we introduce noise, and compare
our approaches under \emph{large} inflation dimensions. 

Taking our results from the experiments on the artificially inflated dimensions, we create a new setup, still using the double
integrator as our underlying structure. Here, we will observe the mass via a simulated camera, and place a number of actuators
evenly distributed among angles on a half-circle. This will give us an actual high-dimensional input, as well as an high-dimensional
output, that is in reality governed by low-dimensional dynamics. 

By inputing images into our existing solvers, the dimensionality increases drastically. To be able to cleanly seperate effects caused
by the non-linearity of the image construction from architecture related effects, we will first artificially inflate our inputs to exactly the size that
the images will be. Also, we set the output dimension to xyz \needcite{TODO}, mimicking the setting we will use for the pixelated
environment. This evaluation will only be done on \emph{ppo_encoder_scalable}, \emph{ppo_mlp} and \emph{ppo_bottleneck}, as the other
approaches either don't scale well or are not relevant to the comparison. 

Then, using the same architectural constructions for these solvers, but applied to our new environment, we compare them with the additionally
introduced Position-velocity Encoder\needcite{Jonschkowski2017PVE}, which uses similar robotic priors to \emph{ppo_encoder_scalable}, but focusses
more on position, velocity and acceleration. It learns positional encodings from the observations, and then calculates the velocity state based
on finite differences. Here, it is quite clear that we use knowledge of the limitations of the embodiment, to construct more optimal controllers,
again in a bow-tie shape.
