\chapter{Robustness of LQR Solvers in Different Dimensions}
\label{chap:lqr}

\section{A Known Model}
\label{sec:known-model}

We start by comparing two approaches that have knowledge of the underlying model,
and thus do not need to estimate gradients but can calculate them exactly. This allows
us to clearly separate the benefits of a low-dimensional controller from estimation
errors.

\subsection{Solvers on the True Model}
\label{subsec:known-model-setting}

% mb pg
Fazel et al.~\cite{Fazel2018Global} introduced global convergence guarantees for policy gradient methods based on the
true model in 2018. The paper describes a method for calculating the gradient of a policy from
the system and cost matrices, and updating the policy accordingly. As the paper uses the same $u_k = -K x_k$
convention as we do, we can implement the loop directly in the following way.

% Def mb_pg
Starting from an initial, stabilizing gain $K_0 \in \mathcal{K}$ and the second moment $\Sigma_0$ of the initial
state distribution, every iteration $j$ first solves the discrete Lyapunov equation \eqref{eq:lyapunov}
for $P_{K_j}$ using the implementation from SciPy~\cite{SciPy2026DiscLyapunov}. The state correlation matrix
$\Sigma_{K_j}$ then follows from solving a second Lyapunov equation,
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:state-correlation}
    \Sigma_{K_j} = (A - B K_j) \Sigma_{K_j} (A - B K_j)\T + \Sigma_0 ,
\end{equation}
where, for initial states drawn uniformly from $[-10, 10] \times [-1, 1]$ as described in \cref{subsec:protocol},
the second moment is $\Sigma_0 = \operatorname{diag}\left( 10^2/3, \, 1^2/3 \right)$. The policy gradient~\cite[Lemma~1]{Fazel2018Global}
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:pg-gradient}
    \nabla f(K_j) = 2 \left( \left( R + B\T P_{K_j} B \right) K_j - B\T P_{K_j} A \right) \Sigma_{K_j}
\end{equation}
is then used for a gradient step on the gain matrix. \Cref{alg:mb-pg} summarizes the loop. We refer to it as \emph{TrP}.
\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
    \caption{Model-based PG}
    \label{alg:mb-pg}
    \begin{algorithmic}[1]
        \REQUIRE stabilizing gain $K_0 \in \mathcal{K}$, second moment $\Sigma_0$, step size $\eta$
        \FOR{$j = 0, 1, 2, \dots$}
            \STATE solve \eqref{eq:lyapunov} for $P_{K_j}$
            \STATE solve \eqref{eq:state-correlation} for $\Sigma_{K_j}$
            \STATE compute the policy gradient $\nabla f(K_j)$ by \eqref{eq:pg-gradient}
            \STATE update the gain by $K_{j+1} = K_j - \eta \nabla f(K_j)$
        \ENDFOR
    \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The fixed learning rate $\eta$ is a hyperparameter, which we set to $\eta = 10^{-5}$ for all inflation sizes, based on
experiments whose results can be found in \cref{sec:app-learning-rate-mb-pg}. This
policy gradient approach serves us as the basic, fully parameterized implementation for this experiment.

It is important to note that the above implementation is described for the uninflated loop. However,
this can be easily applied to our inflated version by replacing every quantity with its inflated counterpart: 
$\tilde{A} = U_1 A U_1\T$, $\tilde{B} = U_1 B U_2$, the inflated weights $\tilde{Q}$, $\tilde{R}$ in
\eqref{eq:inflated-cost}, $\tilde{\Sigma}_0 = U_1 \Sigma_0 U_1\T$ and the initial gain $\tilde{K}_0$ from \eqref{eq:initial-gain},
such that the resulting gain is $K \in \R^{d_{\mathrm{out}} \times d_{\mathrm{in}}}$.

% mb spi

Now we also want to implement a solver that produces a low-dimensional controller. As in our
case the controller is characterized by the gain matrix, and for matrices dimensionality is ``often
[\ldots] expressed by means of the rank''~\cite{Recht2010Guaranteed}, we aim at learning a low-rank
gain matrix. However, to make this comparable later to RL approaches that rely on the number of parameters,
we measure the complexity of a gain by its policy size $\lvert K \rvert$ from \cref{def:policy-size}, which uses
the more efficient version between the full and the factored parameterization.

Recht et al.~\cite{Recht2010Guaranteed} have shown that, given suitable conditions, nuclear norm minimization recovers
low-rank solutions for affine constraints. However, as we are dealing with a non-convex optimization
problem~\cite{Fazel2018Global}, we cannot transfer the results over directly. Here, Park et al.~\cite{Park2020Structured}
offer us an approach to impose different structures on our policy during learning via regularizers
based on norms, one of them being the nuclear norm. We implement the model-based approach introduced there
with small adaptations in the following way.

% Def mb_spi
We reuse the computation of the gradient $\nabla f(K_j)$ from \cref{alg:mb-pg}.
For inducing the structure, we optimize the regularized cost $f(K) + \lambda \lVert K \rVert_{*}$,
where the nuclear norm $\lVert \cdot \rVert_{*}$ can be reduced by eliminating singular values of $K$\cite[Sec.~2.2]{Park2020Structured},
and $\lambda$ is its weight in the whole cost. It further uses an adaptive learning rate,
combined with a line search criterion, consisting of a stability and a sufficient decrease condition.
Starting from the initial step size $\eta = 1 / (2 \lambda)$, following the paper's recommendation of $\mathcal{O}(1/\lambda)$,
each iteration takes a gradient step and applies the
proximal operator of the nuclear norm, which soft-thresholds the singular values,
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:spi-prox}
    K^{+} = \Phi \operatorname{diag} \left( \max\{\chi_1 - \eta \lambda, 0\}, \dots,
    \max\{\chi_{\bar{r}} - \eta \lambda, 0\} \right) \Psi\T ,
    \qquad \bar{r} \coloneqq \min\{d_{\mathrm{out}}, d_{\mathrm{in}}\} ,
\end{equation}
where $\Phi \operatorname{diag}(\chi_1, \dots, \chi_{\bar{r}}) \Psi\T$ is the singular value decomposition of
the gradient step $K_j - \eta \nabla f(K_j)$. The candidate $K^{+}$ is accepted if it is stabilizing, $\spr(A - B K^{+}) < 1$, and
satisfies the sufficient decrease condition
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:spi-sufficient-decrease}
    f(K^{+}) \leq f(K_j) - \eta \tr \left( \nabla f(K_j)\T G \right) + \frac{\eta}{2} \lVert G \rVert_F^2 ,
    \qquad G \coloneqq \frac{1}{\eta} \left( K_j - K^{+} \right) .
\end{equation}
Otherwise, the step size is reduced to $\beta \eta$ and the proximal step is repeated. The learning loop further can be
stopped earlier if the criterion $\lVert K_{j+1} - K_j \rVert_F < \varepsilon$ is satisfied.
\Cref{alg:mb-spi} summarizes the loop, which, run on the true model, we refer to as \emph{TrS}.
\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
    \caption{S-PI}
    \label{alg:mb-spi}
    \begin{algorithmic}[1]
        \REQUIRE stabilizing gain $K_0 \in \mathcal{K}$, second moment $\Sigma_0$, weight $\lambda$, factor $\beta$,
        tolerance $\varepsilon$
        \FOR{$j = 0, 1, 2, \dots$}
            \STATE compute $\nabla f(K_j)$ as in \cref{alg:mb-pg}
            \STATE set the initial step size $\eta = 1 / (2 \lambda)$
            \FOR{at most $20$ line search steps}
                \STATE compute the candidate $K^{+}$ by \eqref{eq:spi-prox}
                \IF{$\spr(A - B K^{+}) < 1$ and \eqref{eq:spi-sufficient-decrease} holds}
                    \STATE \textbf{break}
                \ENDIF
                \STATE reduce the step size, $\eta \leftarrow \beta \eta$
            \ENDFOR
            \STATE update the gain, $K_{j+1} = K^{+}$
            \IF{$\lVert K_{j+1} - K_j \rVert_F < \varepsilon$}
                \STATE \textbf{stop}
            \ENDIF
        \ENDFOR
    \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

As one can see, there are a lot of parameters that can be modified here. We set $\varepsilon = 10^{-8}$,
and use the same $\beta = 0.5$ as in the experiments section of Park et al.~\cite[Sec.~4.2]{Park2020Structured}.
For $\lambda$, as it defines the learning rate as well, we have run an experiment with various values,
which can be found in \cref{sec:app-spi-lambda}. A value of $\lambda = 5$ has shown the most promising results across the sizes.

One thing that we implemented differently than the paper is the cap on the number of line search iterations in line four, which we set to 20.
Some of our previous experiments had shown that if we leave it uncapped, for unstable updates which are impossible
to stabilize, the loop runs until the update produces no effect at all.

Now that we have defined our controllers and how they learn, we test them in comparison to the DARE-calculated
solution \eqref{eq:dare} from \cref{subsubsec:lqr-optimal}.

\section{Unknown Model across Dimensions}
\label{sec:unknown-model}

Now we take a look at the more realistic case, where we do not know the underlying model, and compare different approaches.
Before we can get into the results, we first take a deeper look at the solvers that we use and how the experiments are
set up.

\subsection{System Identification and Model-Free Solvers}
\label{subsec:unknown-model-setting}

First, we introduce our basis for the indirect approaches, which is based on system identification. N4SID,
introduced by Van Overschee and De Moor~\cite{VanOverschee1994N4SID}, uses ``the projection of the future outputs [of the system]
onto the past and future inputs and the past outputs''~\cite[Sec.~3]{VanOverschee1994N4SID}. These projections can then be
described as ``a function of the system matrices and the input-output block Hankel matrices''~\cite[Sec.~3]{VanOverschee1994N4SID}, which
allows us to reconstruct exactly that from the results. Important are further the assumptions that the systems are
observable and controllable~\cite[Sec.~2.1]{VanOverschee1994N4SID}. For simplicity, we rely on the
implementation of N4SID from SIPPY~\cite{SIPPY2026N4SID}, which we integrate into our own setup. This implementation allows us
to recover estimates $A_{\mathrm{id}}$, $B_{\mathrm{id}}$ and $C_{\mathrm{id}}$ of the system matrices, whereas the cost
matrices $\tilde{Q}$ and $\tilde{R}$ are choices of the controller design (\cref{subsec:protocol}), and thus already known beforehand. To the identified matrices
we then apply the following change of basis, based on the QR decomposition $C_{\mathrm{id}} = \hat{C} \Theta$ with
$\hat{C} \in \R^{d_{\mathrm{in}} \times \hat{n}}$ and $\Theta \in \R^{\hat{n} \times \hat{n}}$ upper triangular with a positive diagonal,
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:identified-basis}
    \hat{A} = \Theta A_{\mathrm{id}} \Theta^{-1} , \qquad
    \hat{B} = \Theta B_{\mathrm{id}} , \qquad
    \hat{C} = C_{\mathrm{id}} \Theta^{-1} ,
\end{equation}
such that $\hat{C}$ has orthonormal columns. This makes sense, as we have the knowledge that
the output matrix $C$ of our true model is also of this construction, but also allows us to
get the pseudoinverse of $\hat{C}$ as $\hat{C}\T$, since $\hat{C}\T \hat{C} = I_{\hat{n}}$.

The collection of observation and action data runs the plant for $T$ steps under the initial gain, with a randomly
sampled excitation added on top of it,
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:identification-input}
    v_k = -\tilde{K}_0 y_k + \sigma_{\mathrm{e}} w_k , \qquad w_k \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_{d_{\mathrm{out}}}) ,
    \qquad k = 0, \dots, T - 1 .
\end{equation}

The paper mentions that a part of the result becomes exact as the number of block rows of the Hankel matrices
grows~\cite[Sec.~5.3]{VanOverschee1994N4SID}. With the number of columns being dependent on our trajectory length $T$,
we aim to find the best combination of both. Denoting the number of block rows as the ``Hankel horizon'', we evaluated different
combinations to find the optimal setting to use in our experiment. The full results of the selection can be found in
\cref{sec:app-identification}. Specifically, we use the settings in
\cref{tab:identification-settings}.
\begin{table}[htbp]
    \centering
    \caption{Identification settings per inflation size.}
    \label{tab:identification-settings}
    \begin{tabular}{@{}lrr@{}}
        \toprule
        Name & Trajectory length $T$ & Hankel horizon \\
        \midrule
        \emph{base}   & 500  & 5 \\
        \emph{small}  & 500  & 5 \\
        \emph{medium} & 500  & 5 \\
        \emph{large}  & 2000 & 5 \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

The resulting identified system then serves as a surrogate for the true model
for the model-based approaches from \cref{subsec:known-model-setting}, in which
we assume a latent second moment of $I_{\hat{n}}$, i.e.\ $\tilde{\Sigma}_0 = \hat{C}\hat{C}\T$.
We refer to these as N4SID + Model-based PG (\emph{IdP}) and N4SID + S-PI (\emph{IdS}),
and use for \emph{IdP} an $\eta = 10^{-4}$ and for \emph{IdS}
$\lambda = 1$ across all sizes. This thus produces results without ever having
access to the real model.

An attentive reader might now ask why we would even want to use these iterative approaches, if we could just
solve the DARE \eqref{eq:dare} directly on this estimation. And indeed we include this N4SID + DARE
controller (\emph{IdD}) as an indicator for how well N4SID identifies the system in our following comparisons.

Additionally, we also use direct model-free approaches, which are variations of the model-based approaches
from \cref{sec:known-model}. Unlike before, they do not calculate the gradient directly, but estimate
it based on the returns of $N_{\mathrm{traj}}$ pairs of perturbed versions $K \pm \Xi_i$ of the current policy $K$, each simulated for $l$ steps. As one might
assume, more ``data'' could conversely lead to better estimation and thus better policy updates, but it requires more computation. So, to
settle this, we evaluated different combinations of them in \cref{sec:app-sample-size}. The best
trade-off between computational efficiency and quality gave $N_{\mathrm{traj}} = 200$ and $l = 500$.

% mf npg
We use the model-free natural policy gradient (NPG) estimation described by Fazel et al.~\cite{Fazel2018Global},
which at its core consists of the loop in \cref{alg:mf-gradient}. Instead of the proposed single-point estimator,
we use a two-point estimator inspired by Duchi et al. and Mania et al.~\cite{Duchi2015Optimal,Mania2018Simple},
as this drastically reduced the variance of the estimates, and thus the instability of the controller.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
    \caption{Two-point gradient estimation}
    \label{alg:mf-gradient}
    \begin{algorithmic}[1]
        \REQUIRE gain $K \in \R^{d_{\mathrm{out}} \times d_{\mathrm{in}}}$, number of perturbation pairs $N_{\mathrm{traj}}$,
        rollout length $l$, smoothing radius $\zeta$
        \FOR{$i = 1, \dots, N_{\mathrm{traj}}$}
            \STATE sample $\Xi_i$ uniformly from the matrices with $\lVert \Xi_i \rVert_F = \zeta$
            \STATE sample an initial state $x_0 \sim \mathcal{D}$
            \STATE simulate $K + \Xi_i$ and $K - \Xi_i$ for $l$ steps from $x_0$, with stage costs $c_k^{\pm}$ and
            observations $y_k^{\pm}$
            \STATE $\hat{J}_i^{\pm} = \sum_{k=0}^{l-1} c_k^{\pm}$, \quad
            $\hat{\Sigma}_i^{\pm} = \sum_{k=0}^{l-1} y_k^{\pm} \left( y_k^{\pm} \right)\T$, \quad
            $\delta_i = \hat{J}_i^{+} - \hat{J}_i^{-}$
        \ENDFOR
        \STATE $\bar{\delta} = \frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{traj}}} \sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{traj}}} \delta_i$
        \STATE $\widehat{\nabla f}(K) = \frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{traj}}} \sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{traj}}}
        \frac{d_{\mathrm{out}} d_{\mathrm{in}}}{2 \zeta^2} \left( \delta_i - \bar{\delta} \right) \Xi_i$
        \STATE $\hat{\Sigma}_K = \frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{traj}}} \sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{traj}}} \left( \hat{\Sigma}_i^{+} + \hat{\Sigma}_i^{-} \right)$
        \STATE $\hat{f}(K) = \frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{traj}}} \sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{traj}}} \frac{1}{2} \left( \hat{J}_i^{+} + \hat{J}_i^{-} \right)$
        \RETURN $\widehat{\nabla f}(K)$, $\hat{\Sigma}_K$, $\hat{f}(K)$
    \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

We further aim to clarify a couple of things about the given loop.
Subtracting the mean difference $\bar{\delta}$ here helps us lower the variance of the estimate.
As $\bar{\delta}$ contains $\delta_i$, it scales the expectation by $1 - 1/N_{\mathrm{traj}}$,
which is negligible for our $N_{\mathrm{traj}} = 200$.
We count a rollout as diverged once its observation exceeds
$\lVert y_k \rVert = 10^{5}$. It is therefore ended early and charged a penalty cost of $10^{10}$.
Additionally, for inflated dimensions, $\hat{\Sigma}_K$ is singular, so we precondition with its
truncated pseudoinverse $\hat{\Sigma}_K^{\dagger}$ instead, which discards singular values below $10^{-6}$ times the largest one.

Our full learning algorithm, which we refer to as Model-free NPG (\emph{MfN}), is then given in \cref{alg:mf-npg}.
\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
    \caption{Model-free NPG}
    \label{alg:mf-npg}
    \begin{algorithmic}[1]
        \REQUIRE stabilizing gain $K_0 \in \mathcal{K}$, $N_{\mathrm{traj}}$, $l$, $\zeta$, step size $\eta$
        \FOR{$j = 0, 1, 2, \dots$}
            \STATE compute $\widehat{\nabla f}(K_j)$ and $\hat{\Sigma}_{K_j}$ by \cref{alg:mf-gradient}
            \STATE update the gain by $K_{j+1} = K_j - \eta \, \widehat{\nabla f}(K_j) \, \hat{\Sigma}_{K_j}^{\dagger}$
        \ENDFOR
    \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

We use the natural gradient $\widehat{\nabla f} \, \hat{\Sigma}^{\dagger}$ here, as it
preconditions the update with the pseudoinverse of the state correlation instead of applying
the update directly like the standard model-free version~\cite{Fazel2018Global}. In initial
experiments it consequently showed better stability, which is why we selected it.
In the same process we decided on a smoothing radius of $\zeta = 0.5$, and, based
on separate results in \cref{sec:app-learning-rate-mf-npg}, have chosen a learning rate of $\eta_{\mathrm{base}} = 0.1$,
$\eta_{\mathrm{small}} = 0.03$, $\eta_{\mathrm{medium}} = 0.01$ and $\eta_{\mathrm{large}} = 0.01$.

% spi mf
Next, we implement a model-free version of S-PI (\emph{MfS}) that relies on the same gradient
estimation (\cref{alg:mf-gradient}) as \emph{MfN}. Another crucial difference,
apart from the estimation of the gradient compared to the calculation, is the ``usage of a fixed stepsize
$\eta$, rather than an adaptive stepsize from a backtracking linesearch''~\cite[Sec.~3.1]{Park2020Structured}, as that
would require access to the function value $f(K) = \tr(P_K \Sigma_0)$. Before we look at how we select this step size
and $\lambda$, we define our learning loop in \cref{alg:mf-spi}.
\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
    \caption{S-PI (model-free)}
    \label{alg:mf-spi}
    \begin{algorithmic}[1]
        \REQUIRE stabilizing gain $K_0 \in \mathcal{K}$, $N_{\mathrm{traj}}$, $l$, $r$, fixed step size $\eta$, weight $\lambda$,
        tolerance $\varepsilon$
        \FOR{$j = 0, 1, 2, \dots$}
            \STATE compute $\widehat{\nabla f}(K_j)$ by \cref{alg:mf-gradient}
            \STATE compute $K_{j+1}$ by \eqref{eq:spi-prox}, with $\widehat{\nabla f}(K_j)$ in place of $\nabla f(K_j)$
            \IF{$\lVert K_{j+1} - K_j \rVert_F < 10^{-8}$}
                \STATE \textbf{stop}
            \ENDIF
        \ENDFOR
    \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Now to the selection of the parameters. Park et al.\ already note the difficulty of finding a fixed step size
that does not produce divergence of the method~\cite[Sec.~3.2]{Park2020Structured}. Also, the example shown only
uses the model-based approach and the regularizer to induce sparse structure\cite[Sec.~4]{Park2020Structured}, not the model-free variant combined
with low dimensionality. So during our first tests, we often ran into either unstable controllers, or ones that
did not induce any structure at all, hinting at a $\lambda$ that is too low. If we remember the proximal step
\eqref{eq:spi-prox}, we can see that it depends on both the step size and $\lambda$, as every singular value is
mapped to
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:spi-threshold}
    \chi_i \mapsto \max\{ \chi_i - \eta \lambda, 0 \} .
\end{equation}
Even after some improvement by using the two-point estimator (\cref{alg:mf-gradient}), it remained quite a difficult
topic. So rather extensive experiments on the variation of $\lambda$ and the step size can be found in \cref{sec:app-spi-mf}.
Based on those, we selected $\lambda_{\mathrm{base}} = 4$ and $\eta_{\mathrm{base}} = 3.5 \times 10^{-6}$, $\lambda_{\mathrm{small}} = 400$ and $\eta_{\mathrm{small}} = 3.5 \times 10^{-5}$,
$\lambda_{\mathrm{medium}} = 400$ and $\eta_{\mathrm{medium}} = 3.5 \times 10^{-5}$; and $\lambda_{\mathrm{large}} = 40\,000$ and $\eta_{\mathrm{large}} = 3.5 \times 10^{-8}$.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:lqr-discussion}

From our results, we can see that on the clean loop the identification-based approaches perform much better
than the direct approaches, achieving a performance similar to that of the optimal controller. Here, \emph{IdD} seems to
be the best solution, as it does not require any training iterations, achieves the lowest suboptimality,
and is of a similar size as \emph{IdS}. When looking at our model-free approaches, nothing speaks in favor of them---they
require more iterations and, in the case of \emph{MfS} under \emph{large} inflation, perform poorly on the clean loop, while not even
achieving a strong rank reduction.

This worse performance is then amplified under parametric uncertainty, penalizing them even
more, while \emph{IdD}, \emph{IdP} and \emph{IdS} degrade the same as the baseline. External disturbance affects
all indirect approaches the same, while among the direct approaches, \emph{MfN} shows more
and \emph{MfS} less robustness than those, judging from the absolute degradation.

When looking at control noise, it might seem like this is where \emph{MfS} is much more robust than the
others, and \emph{MfN} slightly as well. But when taking a look at \cref{fig:identified-large-trajectories}, we
can see that \emph{MfN} applies slightly less acceleration than the others, and \emph{MfS} applies it at a
much smaller amplitude. As control noise is in our case multiplicative, a lower value is affected
by much less noise. Therefore, the savings in cost come from the fact that \emph{MfS} is not functioning
well, and does not achieve the goal of steering the mass towards the origin.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \generatedfigure{ch4_identified_model}{ch4_identified_large_trajectories}
  \caption{Position, velocity and control input on the clean loop under \emph{large} inflation.}
  \label{fig:identified-large-trajectories}
\end{figure}

If we extend the same reasoning to measurement noise, it also seems logical that \emph{MfS} is showing less
degradation here. If the gain matrix was not able to control the mass without noise well, hence not
mapping inputs to effective outputs, then a change to these named inputs could have any effect on
the cost. In this case, it seems to help reduce its degradation.

Again, for measurement noise, for \emph{IdS} its slight improvement over \emph{IdP} can be attributed
to a partial removal of the cost associated with the part of the gain located outside the range of
our inflation projection (\cref{sec:app-perf-trp-measurement-noise}). Still, if we account for this,
both perform fairly similarly.

As this system is a very simple case, and system identification is not scalable to larger inflation
(as we will mention in \cref{chap:perception}), we cannot rely on these approaches for real-world applications. We
therefore take these learnings and indications with us, and try to use them for choosing RL-based algorithms
in \cref{chap:inflation}.
