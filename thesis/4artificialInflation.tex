\section{PPO and Its Variants}
\label{sec:ppo-variants}

\subsection{Proximal Policy Optimization}
\label{subsec:ppo}

Before getting into our implementation details, we want to explain the basic construction
of PPO. Introduced by Schulman et al.~\cite{Schulman2017Proximal} in 2017, it is an actor-critic algorithm,
which means it consists of an actor, guided by a policy, that is simulated in an environment,
and a critic, which tries to estimate the underlying value function based on the current policy
and the actor's returns~\cite{Konda2000ActorCritic}. In the case of PPO, as we are trying to optimize a policy
via policy gradient optimization, we ``[construct] an objective function whose gradient is the policy
gradient estimator''~\cite[Sec.~2.1]{Schulman2017Proximal}. What makes PPO special is the construction of this surrogate
function, based on ideas from Trust Region Policy Optimization~\cite{Schulman2015Trust}.
In the paper, two approaches are proposed: a Clipped Surrogate Objective or an Adaptive Kullback--Leibler (KL)
Penalty Coefficient. The former clips the probability ratio past a limit set by a hyperparameter,
imposing constraints on the policy update size~\cite[Sec.~3]{Schulman2017Proximal}, while the latter, rather than setting
a fixed KL for an update, sets a target KL divergence~\cite[Sec.~4]{Schulman2017Proximal}. In case further interest
arises, we refer to the original paper~\cite{Schulman2017Proximal}.
With the probability ratio $r_k(\theta)$ between the current policy $\pi_\theta$ and the policy
$\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}$ that collected the data, the clipped objective becomes
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:ppo-clip}
    \begin{aligned}
        r_k(\theta) &= \frac{\pi_\theta(v_k \mid y_k)}{\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(v_k \mid y_k)} , \\
        L^{\mathrm{CLIP}}(\theta) &= \hat{\E}_k \left[ \min\left\{ r_k(\theta) \hat{A}_k ,\;
            \operatorname{clip}\left( r_k(\theta), 1 - \varepsilon_{\mathrm{clip}}, 1 + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{clip}} \right) \hat{A}_k \right\} \right] .
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Training Setup}
\label{subsec:rl-training}

However, as has been noted before~\cite{Engstrom2020Implementation,Hsu2020Revisiting}, the actual implementation of PPO matters,
and can influence the effectiveness of it in practice. For our construction, we use
the architecture provided by Stable-Baselines3 (SB3)~\cite{StableBaselines2026PPO}.
As the standard architecture, for our Fully-connected PPO (\emph{MLP}), we use the standard two-layer multilayer perceptron (MLP) with 64 neurons
per layer for both the actor and the critic~\cite{StableBaselines2026Policy}. \Cref{tab:app-rl-sb3-parameters} in
\cref{sec:app-rl-parameters} lists all deviations from the default parameters. After evaluating different values, we settled on the
learning rates in \cref{tab:app-rl-learning-rates}, which differ by inflation size.

After initial runs with this basic setup, we could observe some divergence behavior, which
led us to consider additional implementation improvements, as suggested by Huang et al.~\cite{Huang2022Implementation}.
Specifically, we modified the discount factor to $\gamma = 0.999$ instead of $0.99$
and used the reward normalization implementation from SB3~\cite{StableBaselines2026VecNormalize}, based
on a discounted moving average~\cite[continuous-action Detail~8]{Huang2022Implementation}. However, the two most effective changes were:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Using learning-rate annealing instead of a fixed rate~\cite[Detail~4]{Huang2022Implementation}. Now the learning
    rate is decreased with every update, such that at the end of the step budget (\cref{tab:rl-step-caps}) it is equal
    to $0$. So this is
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:lr-annealing}
        \eta(N_{\mathrm{env}}) = \eta_0 \max\left\{ 1 - \frac{N_{\mathrm{env}}}{N_{\max}}, \; 0 \right\} .
    \end{equation}
    \item Separating the feature extractors for actor and critic~\cite[Detail~13]{Huang2022Implementation}. As described
    in the SB3 documentation~\cite{StableBaselines2026Policy}, to reduce computation, in the standard implementation,
    the extractor is shared by default. For extractors that are trained together with PPO, we separate actor and
    critic extractors, such that they are trained separately. Later, when introducing pretrained extractors which are fixed
    post-training, those will be shared again.
\end{enumerate}
\Cref{alg:ppo} summarizes the resulting training loop.
\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
    \caption{PPO implemented here}
    \label{alg:ppo}
    \begin{algorithmic}[1]
        \REQUIRE initial actor $\pi_{\theta}$ and critic $V_{\phi}$, initial learning rate $\eta_0$, step budget $N_{\max}$
        \STATE $N_{\mathrm{env}} \leftarrow 0$
        \WHILE{$N_{\mathrm{env}} < N_{\max}$}
            \STATE run $\pi_\theta$ for $2048$ steps, sampling $v_k \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid y_k)$; store $y_k$, $v_k$ and the rewards
            \STATE normalize the rewards by a running estimate of the return's standard deviation
            \STATE compute the advantages $\hat{A}_k$ and value targets with $V_\phi$
            \STATE $\theta_{\mathrm{old}} \leftarrow \theta$, \quad set the learning rate $\eta(N_{\mathrm{env}})$ by \eqref{eq:lr-annealing}
            \FOR{epoch $= 1, \dots, 10$}
                \FOR{each minibatch of $64$ stored steps}
                    \STATE take an Adam step on $-L^{\mathrm{CLIP}}(\theta) + 0.5 \cdot (\text{value loss of } V_\phi)$,
                    with the gradient norm clipped to $0.5$
                \ENDFOR
                \IF{the approximate KL divergence to $\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}$ exceeds $1.5 \cdot 0.02$}
                    \STATE \textbf{break}
                \ENDIF
            \ENDFOR
            \STATE $N_{\mathrm{env}} \leftarrow N_{\mathrm{env}} + 2048$
            \IF{$25\,000$ steps have passed since the last evaluation}
                \STATE evaluate $\pi_\theta$ for the learning curve
            \ENDIF
        \ENDWHILE
        \STATE \textbf{return} the final policy $\pi_\theta$
    \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Policy Architectures}
\label{subsec:rl-architectures}

Now that we have introduced the base construction, it is easier for us to describe variations of it. We start with
Linear PPO (\emph{Lin}), which allows us to compare the performance of PPO to the previous chapter. Here, our actor becomes a single layer,
with the bias removed, such that we can rewrite this network as
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:linear-ppo}
    \mu(y_k) = W y_k = -K y_k , \qquad K \coloneqq -W \in \R^{d_{\mathrm{out}} \times d_{\mathrm{in}}} ,
\end{equation}
which allows us to extract a gain matrix $K$ that is comparable to the previous chapter. A second, already known, component is
used for State encoder + PPO (\emph{StE}). Here, we reuse an identified system from N4SID (\cref{sec:unknown-model}) to encode an observation into
a latent state:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:state-encoder}
    \hat{x}_k = \hat{C}\T y_k , \qquad \hat{C} \in \R^{d_{\mathrm{in}} \times \hat{n}} , \quad \hat{C}\T \hat{C} = I_{\hat{n}} .
\end{equation}
The orthonormality in the columns of $\hat{C}\T$ is here equal to its pseudoinverse, due to the construction in \cref{subsec:unknown-model-setting}. 
The inputs of both the actor and the critic are thus projected to dimension $\hat{n}$,
which is the order of the identified model.

However, as we will later scale our inputs to larger dimensions (\cref{chap:perception}), N4SID will become infeasible for such dimensions.
Still, we want to stay in line with the idea of encoding our observations into a latent state, as this exactly introduces the bow-tie structure
we want. So, we introduce another encoder, which offers this scalability, and call the combination Scalable encoder + PPO (\emph{ScE}). This feature extractor
is based on the results of Jonschkowski and Brock~\cite{Jonschkowski2015Learning} and Botteghi et al.~\cite{Botteghi2021LowDimensional}, which use robotic priors. Robotic priors, simply described,
are prior knowledge, like physical laws, from which we define loss functions. By adding them to the training process, and optimizing for them, we can
ensure that the learned representation is in line with these laws. As mentioned in \cref{sec:approach}, this allows us to make use of the embodiment
to improve our controller architecture. Here, we will use the following priors~\cite{Botteghi2021LowDimensional}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item simplicity: the relevant information is lower in dimensionality than the observation,
    \item temporal coherence: state changes are rather slow,
    \item proportionality: similar actions should show similar output magnitudes,
    \item repeatability: similar actions in similar states should show similar state changes,
    \item causality: prevents the trivial solution of mapping every observation to the same point.
\end{itemize}

We modify the loss calculations taken from the paper~\cite{Botteghi2021LowDimensional} slightly,
by dropping the $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{reg}}$ term, and thus get the total loss function of
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:prior-loss}
    \mathcal{L} = \omega_1 \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{temp}} + \omega_2 \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{prop}} + \omega_3 \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{rep}}
        + \omega_4 \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{caus}} ,
\end{equation}
with $\omega_1 = \omega_2 = \omega_3 = 1$ and $\omega_4 = 2$.

As this encoder must be trained first, we pretrain it for $20$ epochs on $20\,000$
collected environment steps, before freezing its parameters and prepending
it to the actor and the critic. The data is collected by running a policy using
the initial gain $\tilde{K}_0$ or a randomly drawn action in a $70\,\%$/$30\,\%$ split on
the environment for at most $800$ consecutive steps.

An important parameter is also the dimension of the latent
state, which we set to the actual ``2'' here. Although for later application, one might not know
the real, underlying dimension of the dynamics, we want to make it comparable across the solvers by
using the same low dimension. And as N4SID mostly correctly identifies the true dimension, we use it
here as well. \Cref{alg:encoder-pretraining} summarizes the pretraining.
\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
    \caption{Pretraining of the scalable encoder}
    \label{alg:encoder-pretraining}
    \begin{algorithmic}[1]
        \REQUIRE initial gain $\tilde{K}_0$, action bound $a_{\max}$, encoder $f_\psi \colon \R^{d_{\mathrm{in}}} \to \R^{2}$,
        weights $\omega_1, \dots, \omega_4$
        \FOR{$k = 0, \dots, 20\,000 - 1$}
            \STATE with probability $0.3$, draw $v_k$ uniformly from $[-a_{\max}/10, a_{\max}/10]^{d_{\mathrm{out}}}$,
            otherwise set $v_k = -\tilde{K}_0 y_k$
            \STATE apply $v_k$, store $(y_k, v_k, y_{k+1})$, and reset the plant after $800$ steps
        \ENDFOR
        \FOR{epoch $= 1, \dots, 20$}
            \FOR{each minibatch of $128$ stored transitions}
                \STATE pair every transition with a randomly drawn second transition
                \STATE take an Adam step on $\omega_1 \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{temp}} + \omega_2 \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{prop}}
                + \omega_3 \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{rep}} + \omega_4 \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{caus}}$ of these pairs
            \ENDFOR
        \ENDFOR
        \STATE standardize the latent state with its mean and standard deviation over the stored observations
        \STATE freeze $\psi$ and place $f_\psi$ in front of the actor and the critic
    \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The second trainable extractor we used, in Bottleneck PPO (\emph{OrB}), is based on the paper by Todorov and Sabatelli~\cite{Todorov2026Orthogonal},
using orthogonal bottlenecks. It consists of a fully connected MLP, with 512 neurons in the first,
and 256 neurons in the second and third layer, using a ReLU activation function.
Following it is a matrix with orthonormal columns, which projects the output
into a state of bottleneck dimension $d_{\mathrm{b}}$, which we here set to $2$. Like $U_1$ in \eqref{eq:lifts}, the matrix
is the orthonormal factor of the QR decomposition of a random matrix with standard normal entries, so that the
feature extractor output $z_k \in \R^{d_{\mathrm{z}}}$ is mapped to
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:ortho-bottleneck}
    U_{\mathrm{b}}\T z_k \in \R^{d_{\mathrm{b}}} ,
    \qquad U_{\mathrm{b}} \in \R^{d_{\mathrm{z}} \times d_{\mathrm{b}}} , \quad U_{\mathrm{b}}\T U_{\mathrm{b}} = I_{d_{\mathrm{b}}} .
\end{equation}

Unlike the previous encoder, this one is trained together with PPO, so the actor and critic train them separately, while
the projection matrix $U_{\mathrm{b}}$ stays the same for both.

Lastly, we introduce FC-Extractor + PPO (\emph{FcE}), which uses the same three layer extractor, but passes its $256$-dimensional
output to the actor and the critic directly, without projecting it via $U_{\mathrm{b}}$.

\Cref{fig:ppo-architectures} in \cref{sec:app-ppo-architectures} visualizes the architectures of all our solvers.

\section{Comparison across Dimensions}
\label{sec:rl-comparison}

Looking at achieved suboptimality during training, we overall see a learning process in almost all
of our architectures (\cref{fig:rl-large-curves}). Noticeable, however, is the long stagnation in the beginning of the learning,

improvement under \emph{large}, but this was expected, given its unsuccessful learning-rate search results
(\cref{fig:app-rl-learning-rate-ppo-linear-large}). Also, when looking at the spread, we see that in some seeds

Looking at the complexity of our trained policies, we can see that \emph{StE}

The group of much larger policies (10x to 15x) consists of \emph{OrB}, the non-dimensionally reduced \emph{FcE}

Regarding the performance, we can see that under \emph{small} inflation \emph{Lin}, \emph{OrB}
and \emph{FcE} achieve a similar median,
while \emph{StE} and \emph{MLP} have a slightly worse performance,
with \emph{ScE} being the worst of them all. However, when looking at the spreads,
\emph{StE} has some runs in the $100$ to $1000$ suboptimality range, which are the largest ones, while
the worst case of \emph{MLP} is similar to that of \emph{FcE}. The best one here
is \emph{Lin}, which shows little spread.

Looking at \emph{medium}, here \emph{MLP} becomes worse regarding its median, followed by \emph{ScE}, even though
the spread of \emph{ScE} still has worse runs. \emph{FcE} maintains its median from
\emph{small}, only increasing its spread a bit, making it the best of them all. The other low-dimensional
controllers show a good median similar to \emph{Lin}, however have a wider spread,
with some runs worse than $100$ suboptimality.

Now under \emph{large} it becomes interesting, as the performance of \emph{Lin} has deteriorated, becoming the
worst controller of them all, judging from the median. Under worst-case evaluation, it is still the best.
\emph{ScE} is still second, now again worse than the fully connected version.
However, the other large-parameter architectures,
\emph{OrB} and \emph{FcE}, show a similar performance as under \emph{medium}, while most others
(except for \emph{MLP}) have shifted upwards. The simple \emph{StE} also remains in a good spot,
even though it has worsened in comparison to \emph{OrB} (\cref{fig:rl-across-sizes-subopt}).

\section{Noise on Large Inflation}
\label{sec:rl-noise-large}

% ---- Parametric Uncertainty --------

Similar to what we have seen before in \cref{subsec:noise-large-inflation}, parametric uncertainty
seems to affect most trained controllers similarly and generally increases suboptimality by multiple orders
of magnitude. Here, the baseline remains the most robust one, with only its calibrated increase by a factor of $10$
under \emph{divergent}, while for the majority it is one between $10^5$ and $10^6$ in the median case. However,
\emph{Lin} seems to be more robust than the other trained policies, ``only'' increasing by $10^3$. When only looking
at \cref{fig:rl-noise-large-parametric-divergent}, one might question whether \emph{Lin} is actually
more robust than the others, given its worse performance on the nominal plant. A small relative increase could
then still mean a large absolute one. However, checking \cref{fig:rl-noise-large-parametric-absolute},
we notice that indeed it also degrades less than the other controllers absolutely.

% ------- External Disturbance --------

For external disturbance, not much can be said, as here all controllers are affected relatively and absolutely in a similar
fashion. The only noticeable difference is a wider range of values for \emph{Lin}, with two noticeably worse seeds pushing
its absolute degradation above the others. However, for \emph{Lin} and \emph{ScE}, there also exists one seed
each which experiences only a fourfold relative cost increase, compared to the tenfold increase measured at the baseline.

% -------- Control noise ---------

Under control noise, we can observe that trained controllers degrade in relation to how well they performed on the nominal
loop. This can be seen from the fact that the order of relative degradation in \cref{fig:rl-noise-large-control-divergent} is the same as for the clean plant.
Generally, however, we can see that all trained controllers except \emph{Lin} are more robust on average than the baseline in relative terms (\cref{fig:rl-noise-large-control-relative}).
In absolute terms, \emph{ScE} shows a worse degradation, which mainly stems from its wide spread of degradation in relation
to its seeds. Actually, in three out of the ten seeds, it degrades less than the other controllers (excluding \emph{Lin}), but then
in three others it shows more degradation, making it overall worse on average. More robust are
\emph{StE} and \emph{MLP}, as well as \emph{OrB} and \emph{FcE}, showing slightly better
robustness properties for the larger controllers.
