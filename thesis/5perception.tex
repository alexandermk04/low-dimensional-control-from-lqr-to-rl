\chapter{Perception-Based Experiments}
\label{chap:perception}

From the previous artificially inflated inputs, we aim to move to a more realistic
example, which shows the robustness of low-dimensional controllers in a scenario involving
an image observation as input. We do this by first using our setup from the previous
chapter to scale it to the dimension of the images (\cref{sec:pixel-inflation}). Afterwards, we
build a new environment (\cref{sec:pixel-env}), which combines perception-based observation with
multiple actuators acting on our double integrator, before comparing the controllers' performance
on it (\cref{sec:visual-inputs}). Finally, we again apply noise and analyze its effects (\cref{sec:perception-noise}).

\section{Artificial Inflation to Pixel Size}
\label{sec:pixel-inflation}

\subsection{Scaling to Pixels}
\label{subsec:pixel-inflation-setting}

As mentioned, we take our loop from before (\cref{subsubsec:inflated-model}) and scale it up further. The input
dimension in this case becomes $18\,432$, while we set the output dimension to $256$. These numbers
were specifically selected to match the dimensions in \cref{sec:pixel-env}, such that we can
compare both more closely. For the input dimension, this is a $96 \times 96$ image stack of two images,
and for the output dimension, the number of thrusters. The goal is to separate effects caused by image observation from
the increased dimensions later in \cref{sec:visual-inputs}.

We further note that \emph{StE} cannot be applied at this scale, as the identification
for such large matrices is computationally infeasible, since the matrix decompositions would not fit into
memory. Additionally, we excluded \emph{Lin}, due to its already weak performance under \emph{large}.

Due to computational complexity and time restrictions, we reduced the training steps to $10^{7}$ and
did not run experiments to determine the optimal learning rate here. Instead, we used the optimal
learning rate for \emph{large}, and extrapolated it from our known values via
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:pixel-learning-rate}
    \eta_0^{\mathrm{pixel}}
    = \eta_0^{\mathrm{large}} \sqrt{\frac{200}{18\,432}}
    \approx 0.104\, \eta_0^{\mathrm{large}} ,
\end{equation}
rounded to two significant digits as in \eqref{eq:app-rl-learning-rate-grid}, which leaves us with the values in \cref{tab:pixel-learning-rates}.

\section[The New Environment and the Position-Velocity Encoder]{The New Environment and the\\Position-Velocity Encoder}
\label{sec:pixel-env}

\subsection{Pixelated Environment}
\label{subsec:pixelated-environment}

For our perception setup, we still use the underlying double integrator as a basis, however now
simulate its actual movement. This simulation is observed by a monochromatic camera producing $96 \times 96$-pixel
images of a mass with a radius of two pixels moving horizontally in front of a black background. The visible range
is from $-12$ to $12$ meters, so a total of $24$ meters, corresponding to $0.25$ meters per pixel. To be able to see
changes between the pictures, we hold any action commanded by the agent for $30$ environment steps before
capturing another frame and call this interval an \emph{agent step}.
The agent then receives a stack of two images as the input. \Cref{fig:pixel-setup-observation} shows
an example of such an image.

In addition to the input being high-dimensional compared to the actual state, we also modify the way
our mass is actuated. We replace the single actuator with $256$ thrusters, distributed equally around
a half-circle, so thruster $i \in \{0, \dots, 255\}$ sits at an angle of $\pi i / 256$. Each thruster can actuate in the
range $(v_k)_i \in [-1, 1]$; however, due to the angle, only $b_i \coloneqq b_{\max} \cos(\pi i / 256)$ effective acceleration is produced on
the mass, where $b_{\max}$ is a scaling factor.%
\footnote{The most energy-efficient and thus cheapest way to produce an acceleration $u_k$ is the least-norm allocation, which
uses the thrusters based on their output effectiveness $(v_k)_i = u_k \cos(\pi i / 256)/(128 \cdot b_{\max})$, where $128 = \sum_i \cos^2(\pi i / 256)$.
The horizontal, and thus most effective, thruster saturates at $u_k = 128 \cdot b_{\max}$. We thus choose $b_{\max}$ here such that $1.5$ times the maximum acceleration demanded
by our optimal controller can be reached without clipping, which is $26\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$, so $b_{\max} = 26/128$.}

This leads to some thrusters being more effective than others, while all are charged for the commanded output,
so some energy might be lost. For example, the only vertically acting thruster $i = 128$ cannot
affect the mass in any way, and would thus only waste energy if used. \Cref{fig:pixel-setup-thrusters}
visualizes the thrust directions for eight thrusters.

With our chosen frame and action repeat, a pixel difference between two frames can only be guaranteed at velocities
above $0.83\,\mathrm{m/s}$. Otherwise, motion is only visible if the mass crosses a pixel boundary, making velocity
generally hard to identify. For a better approximation, the number of frames stacked could be increased,
or anti-aliasing could be used to display changes better.

\subsection{Position-Velocity Encoder}
\label{subsec:pve}

For this new environment, we introduce another feature extractor, which is based on robotic priors used
in \emph{ScE}~\cite{Jonschkowski2015Learning,Botteghi2021LowDimensional}. Called Position-Velocity Encoders~\cite{Jonschkowski2017PVE},
it tries to encode a position and velocity state from an observation, by learning a latent position state, and then
reconstructing the velocity state using temporal differences. As we specifically look for position and velocity in our
states, we can construct even more specific priors, again in line with our reasoning that knowledge about the embodiment
helps in designing the controller architecture (\cref{sec:approach}). The priors used in~\cite[Sec.~III-B]{Jonschkowski2017PVE} are:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Variation: ``Positions of relevant things vary.''
    \item Slowness: ``Positions change slowly.''
    \item Inertia: ``Velocities change slowly.''
    \item Conservation: ``Velocity magnitudes change slowly.''
    \item Controllability: ``Controllable things are relevant.''
\end{enumerate}

For our implementation, we follow the paper's recommended approach. Each frame is input
into a convolutional neural network (CNN), which encodes a latent position state $\hat{p}_k$
from it. Its dimension is a hyperparameter, but is set to five throughout the paper~\cite[Sec.~III-C]{Jonschkowski2017PVE},
which we follow. The velocity is then calculated as the difference between two frames,
or $\hat{\dot{p}}_k = \alpha \left(\hat{p}_k - \hat{p}_{k-1}\right)$, where
$\alpha$ is the inverse of the time between two agent steps. In the choice of $\alpha$ we deviated from the paper for simplicity. 
For the weighting of our prior loss terms, we use the combination suggested for similar tasks in~\cite[Tab.~I]{Jonschkowski2017PVE}, and thus
exclude the controllability prior. For the training process we further follow the one proposed in~\cite[Sec.~III-C]{Jonschkowski2017PVE}.
For details on how exactly the total loss function is constructed, we refer the reader to the original paper~\cite{Jonschkowski2017PVE}.
We train this extractor with data of $20\,000$ agent steps, which is split into episodes of $810$ environment steps,
which at our action repeat corresponds to $27$ agent steps. Similar to before, we use $K_0$ acting on the true
state in $70\,\%$ with least-norm allocation through the thrusters and random, uniformly distributed actuation drawn from
$[-1, 1]^{256}$ in $30\,\%$ of the time,
before freezing the extractor's weights and using it in front of our PPO construct (\cref{sec:ppo-variants}).
We refer to it as Position-Velocity Encoder + PPO (\emph{PVE}).

\emph{ScE} is pretrained with a similar data split for this setting.

\section{Effect of Visual Inputs on the Solvers}
\label{sec:visual-inputs}

\subsection{Reference Controller and Solvers}
\label{subsec:visual-inputs-setting}

For the experiments here, we use the optimal gain under our action repeat (\cref{subsec:pixelated-environment})
as our reference and compare it with different approaches,
which here consist of \emph{FcE}, \emph{ScE}, \emph{OrB} as well as our
\emph{PVE} (\cref{subsec:pve}). As they reuse the already introduced architectures,
they will not be repeated here. The only difference is that we increase the latent dimension of \emph{ScE}
to $5$, which is the same as \emph{PVE}, after initial results showed a weak performance with $2$.
The optimal controller for this setup actually does not
act based on the perception, but instead has access to the real state, while the output
remains our thrusters, for which it uses the least-norm allocation.
It is therefore optimal, and serves as the lower bound for our comparison. Given the knowledge it has about the true
state, we call it ``Oracle (true state)'' in our visualizations.
For the other approaches, except for \emph{PVE}, which takes images
as an input, we flatten the images, such that they receive an $18\,432$-dimensional input vector.
The learning rates we use here are identical to the ones extrapolated in \cref{tab:pixel-learning-rates}.
As we did not use \emph{PVE} (\cref{subsec:pve}) before, we copy the learning rate from \emph{ScE} for it, as it is the
most similar (both use robotic priors).

However, we also modify some of our PPO parameters for this setting. Parameters not mentioned
keep their values from \cref{chap:inflation}. Specifically, we
\begin{itemize}
    \item set $\gamma = 0.97$, which gives us around $1/(1-\gamma) \approx 33$ effective agent steps.
    Multiplying this with our agent step interval of $0.3\,\mathrm{s}$ gives us roughly $10\,\mathrm{s}$
    of effective horizon, equal to what we used before;
    \item raise the reward clipping threshold from $10$ to $\infty$ (\cref{subsec:rl-training}), so the reward is never clipped;
    \item charge the input cost on the command before clipping, though only enact the clipped command;
    \item end the episode with a penalty if the mass exits the observable area. The penalty is set to twice the
    cost that a mass sitting at the edge of the screen would incur over a whole episode.
\end{itemize}
The last three points relate a bit to each other, and were changes that were implemented after initial
test failures. Here, we noticed that by charging the cost only after clipping, the controller
never learned to actually control the mass in some cases. For these, it would produce increasingly larger
command inputs, but could never return from this instability, as every action above this threshold had the
same effect. Also, clipping the reward like in \cref{chap:inflation} had the side effect that leaving the
screen became an economically reasonable choice, so the model chose this behavior more often. Combined with
this, we started charging the penalty, which promotes staying on screen even more.

We also need to slightly adapt our test protocol (\cref{subsec:protocol}) to this new environment.
To maintain similarity to the previous experiments, we choose $27$ agent steps per episode during training,
corresponding to $810$ environment steps. The overall training step budget is chosen as $10^{7}$ agent steps.
For one test episode we use $\lceil 2000/30 \rceil = 67$ agent steps, corresponding to $2010$ environment steps.

\subsection{Additional Metrics}
\label{subsec:visual-inputs-metrics}

With an increase in environment complexity, it becomes increasingly difficult to trace back performance
to certain behaviors. To change that, we introduce two additional metrics for this chapter, which allow
us to assess the quality of the encoded latent state, as well as a controller's ability to actuate effectively.

Inspired by Jonschkowski et al.~\cite[Sec.~IV-C]{Jonschkowski2017PVE} and Lesort et al.~\cite[Sec.~4.3]{Lesort2018State},
we implement a regression-based probe on the latent state.
To not replicate the exact setup from the PVE paper~\cite[Sec.~IV-C]{Jonschkowski2017PVE}, which uses a neural network as the probe,
we instead rely on a stricter, yet simpler, construction similar to the one used by Alain and Bengio~\cite{Alain2017Understanding}. We use
ridge regression, which keeps the system well conditioned for strongly correlated predictors~\cite{Hoerl1970Ridge}, and collect pairs
of the true and the latent state over $20$ episodes with $27$ agent steps each post-training. We train with a ridge of $10^{-3}$
on the first $14$ episodes of the data, before computing the coefficient of determination on the steps $\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{test}}$ of the remaining six episodes,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:probe-r2}
  R^2 = 1 - \frac{\sum_{k \in \mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{test}}} (\xi_k - \hat{\xi}_k)^2}{\sum_{k \in \mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{test}}} (\xi_k - \bar{\xi})^2},
\end{equation}
where $\xi_k$ is the true value, $\hat{\xi}_k$ the prediction from the latent state and $\bar{\xi}$ its mean over the training steps.
Similarly, we compute it for control, using the command from the oracle as the target.
Overall, an $R^2 = 1$ tells us that the latent state has encoded this feature perfectly linearly,
while a value close to $0$ means that it is not better than just predicting the training mean.

A second metric we use is wasted energy via the control input. As mentioned in \cref{subsec:pixelated-environment}, the resulting actuation $u_k$
is calculated via $u_k = b\T v_k$, where $v_k$ is the commanded actuation, and $b \coloneqq [\,b_0, \dots, b_{255}\,]\T$, due to
the construction of the thrusters. We compare the least-norm allocation $u_k\, b/\lVert b\rVert^2$ with the commanded input, to determine what
amount of energy is directed into the null space of $b\T$, and thus never reaches the mass. The resulting equation, calculated for each step, is
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:null-waste}
  \lVert v_k\rVert^2 - \frac{u_k^2}{\lVert b\rVert^2},
\end{equation}
which is then averaged over the steps of all $100$ episodes.

Calculating a control input from these is then probably delegated to the PPO network, and the extractor is therefore mainly an encoder.
For \emph{OrB}, we notice a difference here: the latent state partially encodes the oracle's control ($0.68$),

of the test episodes, getting charged the exit penalty (\cref{fig:perc-off-screen}).
Encoding-wise, \emph{PVE} captures the position of its point almost perfectly, and
indicates well how the controller should steer the mass. But, as in the previous cases, and here most
severely, the complexity of PPO seems to be too reduced to effectively translate it onto the thrusters. Judging
by the major waste in the null space, \emph{PVE} cannot differentiate well between effective and wasteful
thrusters, and therefore does not move at all in some cases (\cref{fig:app-perc-trajectories}). As a result, it also
loses the mass in $3.1\,\%$ of the episodes. So, for both \emph{ScE} and \emph{PVE}, a higher suboptimality
than \emph{FcE} or \emph{OrB} is produced, where the latter do not exit any episode early.

As a summary, we note that a low-dimensional latent layer can indeed produce effective encodings of the
true state, even with far fewer parameters. However, the complexity of the following network must
allow this encoding to be translated back into action, which in our case it fails to do, possibly because
of its size. This effect is even more pronounced if the extractor is frozen, and thus both parts are trained separately, and the
majority of the training happens only on $0.3\,\%$ (\emph{ScE}) or $1.7\,\%$ (\emph{PVE}) of the parameters. Increasing the complexity of
the actor until performances comparable to \emph{FcE} can be achieved is left for future research.

\subsection{Robustness of Perception-Based Control}
\label{subsec:perception-noise-results}

As we have already noted in \cref{subsec:visual-inputs-results}, \emph{ScE} and \emph{PVE} perform far worse than
\emph{FcE} and \emph{OrB}, and are barely acting on the mass. We keep this in mind for our analysis of the results,
and drop relative degradation as a metric for this section, as it is heavily skewed.

Looking now at parametric uncertainty, we do notice that \emph{FcE} and \emph{OrB} seem to be more robust under lower
intensities, but sharply increase in absolute degradation from \emph{standard} onwards, while \emph{PVE} and \emph{ScE}
already increase a lot under \emph{weak}, but then remain relatively stable (\cref{fig:perc-noise-parametric-absolute}).
While initially suspected to indicate robustness, the plateauing is actually caused by penalties for leaving the screen.
Under \emph{weak}, for \emph{ScE} the mass exits $52\,\%$ of the time, for \emph{PVE} it sits at $36\,\%$
(\cref{tab:perc-noise-terminated}). As under \emph{divergent} those values sit
at $74\,\%$ and $62\,\%$ respectively, we can attribute this cap on the increase to this.

If we take a look at external disturbance now, we can also see that \emph{PVE} seems to be similarly
affected as \emph{FcE} and \emph{OrB} under lower intensities, with \emph{ScE} performing worse, while under \emph{divergent}
our largely pretrained controllers outperform the others (\cref{fig:perc-noise-disturbance-absolute}). What is generally noticeable is that the
oracle does outperform all of them; however, this is expected, as it is not affected by the high complexity
of the environment.

Looking at the effect of control noise again, we find support for our previous hypothesis that
\emph{PVE} does not manage to correctly address its thrusters. Similarly to \emph{ScE}, which wastes
more thrust in the null space than the other architectures, this leads to a higher increase in absolute degradation
under control noise, as overall more control is applied (\cref{fig:perc-noise-control-absolute}). Again, we
notice overall that the larger architectures handle control noise much better than the smaller ones. For
\emph{OrB} compared with \emph{FcE}, the influence of only encoding the control signal in its latent state
also seems to produce an adverse effect.

Taking now a look at measurement noise, it is quite difficult to interpret the result. On the one hand,
the lower effect of it on \emph{ScE} and the flattening of the curve for \emph{PVE} (\cref{fig:perc-noise-measurement-absolute}) could be an indicator that
those low-dimensional controllers are less affected by measurement noise, which is something we suspected from the results in our previous chapters. However,
this is not the case here. Instead, for \emph{ScE} it is the effect we noticed before, where controllers
that are already non-functional on the clean plant are generally less affected by noise on their inputs. For \emph{PVE},
any noise intensity applied leads to a high percentage of the mass exiting the screen ($18$, $43$, $73$ and $79\,\%$, \cref{tab:perc-noise-terminated}),
which consequently ends the episode early with our previously discussed penalty (\cref{subsec:visual-inputs-setting}).
As a result, the curve flattens at the higher intensities, as the cost cannot increase much more.
Therefore, as \emph{FcE} and \emph{OrB} do not show any difference under \emph{divergent}, there is no establishable basis for the
claim that low-dimensional latents improve robustness against measurement noise, at least for this setting.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:perception-discussion}

Concluding this chapter, we first of all note that, on the clean loop, the fully connected approach, \emph{FcE},
is unbeaten in terms of performance, and a low-dimensional latent layer as in \emph{OrB} does not show any improvement;
instead, it performs worse. This was already prominent under our pixel-size inflation, and has now
persisted in our visual input example. Similarly, we also were not able to effectively implement \emph{ScE}, such that
it could show strong improvements over the other controllers. While it still functioned under \emph{large}, even though worse
than the other controllers, with only a fraction of trainable parameters, under visual input it became practically useless, most likely due to
its inability to encode velocity. \emph{PVE}, however, does encode velocity ($R^2 \approx 0.68$), and position and control
even better than \emph{FcE}, with a much smaller extractor. A previously mentioned increase in the number of frames stacked
or improved imaging (anti-aliasing, \cref{subsec:pixelated-environment}) could potentially improve these encodings for \emph{FcE}.
In the end, the actor head failed to translate those encodings into the correct actions,
which hints at the PPO network being too small, if the extractor is frozen before. This could have also contributed to
\emph{ScE}'s worse performance.

Looking at the effect of noise, we again notice that parametric uncertainty affects already worse-performing
controllers generally more. For external disturbances, we observe a pattern under \emph{divergent} intensity, where \emph{ScE} and \emph{PVE} both
degrade noticeably less than \emph{FcE} and \emph{OrB}, which are not able to contain the mass in most of the episodes (\cref{tab:perc-noise-terminated}). A possible robustness
stemming from the frozen extractors could be the case, but cannot be clearly attributed based on our data.
Under control noise, we find that controllers which cannot coordinate their thrusters well, and waste more thrust,
are consequently more affected by it. Also, the trend that fully parameterized controllers are less affected by it
continues in our experiments. Finally, we notice, contrary to our previous findings, that controllers carrying a low-dimensional latent state
do not show more robustness to measurement noise, as the only lower absolute degradation of \emph{ScE} can be explained by ineffectiveness of the
controller on the clean plant.

Overall, based on our observations in this chapter, controllers containing a low-dimensional latent state,
as well as far fewer trainable parameters, can be constructed using pretrained extractors. Making them perform
at suboptimalities similar to fully connected controllers, however, requires a careful selection of the actor size,
as well as an effective encoding of all relevant properties. The latter is especially what matters when it comes
to robustness. If both are fulfilled, it is probable that such a controller can outperform a full network, while being
more space efficient. This, however, remains to be shown.
